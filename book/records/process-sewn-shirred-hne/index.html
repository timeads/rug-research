Research notes

How it works: in chenille shirring, a fabric strip is stitched lengthwise down the middle and gathered tight into a caterpillar-like strand, which is sewn flat onto the foundation along the pattern. In bias shirring, short bias-cut strips are sewn on edge (Historic New England; Rug Hooking Magazine). Rug Hooking Magazine says shirring was most common in 1820–1850. Conflict: Rugmaker's Homestead dates shirred rugs to about 1870, but museum-dated examples are earlier. This object: polychrome wool on a brown and black ground with a narrow blue border, described with the term 'shirring'. Gift of Bertram K. and Nina Fletcher Little. Other examples: HNE 1991.1519 (shirred, 1825–1850), Winterthur 'Elephant' rug (Rhode Island, 1810–1825, bias and chenille shirring on cotton), and a bias-shirred hearth rug, probably Maine, ca. 1830 (Caputo collection, Colonial Williamsburg exhibition).
