Research notes

10-minute training video. Hagley summary: "From chemicals to yarn. An in-depth look at the creation of superpolymer Type 66 Nylon, a synthetic fiber made for use in commercial carpets. Details how scientists manipulate specific compounds and apply heat and pressure to create the fiber, and why it's strong enough to withstand a large amount of wear and tear. Released by The Learning Center, DuPont Flooring Systems." Why it matters: Nylon chapter: DuPont's own explanation of how its nylon 6,6 carpet fibre was made, useful for describing the fibre side of tufted carpet. Late (1991), so background rather than evidence for the 1950s questions. Found: Hagley Digital Archives, search "carpet fiber", 2026-10-28. From the transcript (Hagley caption file, lightly cleaned; the captions skip the first two minutes and part of the closing recap; transcript added 2 Nov 2026): - [2:14] The film is set at "the DuPont Company fiber manufacturing plant at Seaford, Delaware, as at four other DuPont fiber plants worldwide": adipic acid arrives by hopper cars and hexamethylene diamine by tank cars, and is pumped to an autoclave, "like a giant pressure cooker". - Four major steps: polymerization, extrusion, drawing and texturizing (crimping). - Polymerization: in the autoclave the raw materials are linked into "linear superpolymers". At this molten stage luster, opacity (for styling and soil-hiding) and dye affinity are set. Fibres of different dye affinities combined in an undyed carpet give "multiple colors and patterns" in one dye bath. Pigment added to the melt makes "solution-dyed nylon", with color "locked into the fiber itself". - Extrusion: molten polymer is pumped through a spinneret, "similar in principle to a shower head"; the holes set the size and cross-section of the filaments. In commercial carpet larger filaments are generally preferred, to minimise the surface soil clings to. The narrator says the "modified square and delta-shaped filaments are available only from DuPont" and are "engineered to best diffuse light to hide soil". - Staple and bulked continuous filament (BCF): staple filaments are gathered into a rope or tow, crimped in a two-dimensional zigzag, cut into short lengths, baled and shipped to carpet mills or yarn processors to be spun, plied and heat-set; BCF filaments are drawn, crimped into a "curvilinear, three-dimensional texture" and wound onto packages in one continuous process, a single package holding about 30 miles of yarn. BCF is used in all types of carpet construction; spun staple in constructions that are mainly or entirely cut pile. - Drawing stretches the fibre to align its molecules for strength. Crimping under heat gives texture and bulk, and nylon 6,6 keeps "a memory of its crimp". - Anti-static filaments are added to "every DuPont nylon carpet fiber bundle", reducing static "below the sensitivity levels of humans and most office electronics". - Closing: Antron type 6,6 nylon "remains the ideal fiber where styling flexibility and appearance retention qualities are of prime importance". The film is DuPont's own account of its product, made to sell it to the commercial carpet trade.
