Research notes

How it works: a two-stage weave. First a 'weft blanket' is woven in the pattern colours and cut into strips. Each strip is folded into a furry 'chenille' (caterpillar) yarn and then woven into a backing as the pile weft. This gives seamless, Axminster-like cut pile without hand-tying every knot (GSA Archives; Wikipedia). History: the GSA Archives say Templeton and Quiglay, a weaver he employed, patented an improved chenille method in July 1839. Templeton saw its use for carpet and in December 1839 bought out Quiglay's share. Conflicts: Wikipedia calls Quiglay a fellow shawl maker and dates their contact to 1837. A Glasgow paper calls him an Irish weaver, and another account places the chenille experiments in Paisley. This object: a late-19th-century Templeton stair-carpet sample with a Persian palmette and cloud-band design, pile woven in chenille strips, 84.5 × 66.7 cm. See also GSA DC 077/8 (Templeton 'Special Seamless Axminster' stair runner, c. 1880s–1930s; not confirmed as chenille).
