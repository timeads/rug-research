Research notes

Audio interview, 52 minutes, 1974, from the John Burrison Georgia Folklore Archives at the Atlanta History Center (via the Digital Library of Georgia, https://dlg.usg.edu/record/geh_p17222coll18_585). Atlanta History Center item p17222coll18/585. Part two is u-mvyjj0jgz1c5k. Speakers: the archive names the interviewer as Marguerite Stewart and the interviewees as Burl Jackson "Jack" Bandy (1926-2020) and Altha Sue Quinton Gordon (1928-2011). In the transcripts a woman called "Joy" both asks Bandy questions (part one) and answers questions about learning to hand-tuft (part two); she is probably Altha Sue Quinton Gordon, but the recording does not say so. Bandy speaks of his own parents as early spread makers; that he is the Dalton carpet manufacturer for whom the Bandy Heritage Center is named is likely but not stated in the interview. The transcripts were made from the audio and published to the research tool on 2 Nov 2026; part two repeats several passages, apparently transcription artefacts. From the transcript (part one: Jack Bandy): - Mrs. Whitener "is known really as the mother of the tufted industry". He places her start "right after the turn of the century" and around the First World War; she made hand-tufted spreads, "a mountain craft", and was the first to make them to sell. "Mrs. Whitener actually just continued to do her thing primarily, and never was considered to be financially successful in the industry, but certainly everyone respected her." - His father, hearing of it, said: "Well gee, if Mrs. Whitener can do that, I don't know why we can't do it." His mother went to see Mrs. Whitener, who gave her some of her patterns. - Patterns were full-size: a clean sheet was laid over the pattern and marked over with a coloring crayon or block, then tufted with needle and thread. - His parents contracted with local women, "country wives, and mothers", paid by piece rate; the women collected stamped sheets and yarn at weekends and brought finished spreads back. He guessed "15 cents or 20 cents to sew up a spread". About half a dozen people started at the same time; "spread houses" soon carried sheets and yarn "within probably a 30-mile radius". - Finished spreads were boiled in open vats of 40 or 50 gallons over open fires and stirred with sticks; the cotton sheeting shrank and held the tufts, and the cut cotton ends of the 10- or 11-ply yarn bloomed out. With no dryers, spreads were hung on a line, beaten with a spring beater and rubbed with cane poles to fluff them. "That was really a hand industry." - The market was department stores around the country. His father was a telegraph operator on the Southern Railway and taught his mother telegraphy; she had been a school teacher. She did the selling, travelling by train on railroad passes to Washington, Philadelphia and New York with samples in a suitcase; on her first trip, to Washington, the first store she called on "bought everything they could make for the next month, I think it was". His father ran the manufacturing. - The trade stayed strong through the Depression. He estimated the hand ("candlewick") industry at "$30 million to $40 million ... maybe a $25 to $30 million". - "Along about 1936", as he put it, the Wage and Hour Law required a minimum wage in interstate commerce; home piece-work could not meet it, "So they started trying to develop machines to do this." - A man named Boyce of Gastonia, North Carolina, made a machine-made "chenille" product on a sewing machine; Bandy's father worked with him to adapt it to "duplicate a hand-tufted look", a "skip-stitch" machine, then bought his patents and company. Bandy calls Boyce "the father of the mechanical age of the tufted industry" and credits him with the Boyce Knotter. - Patents were hardly enforced; there were suits and counter-suits that were dropped. "There must have been 100 people trying to" build a better machine, with little machine shops everywhere. - Single-needle machines became two-needle, then 100-needle machines, based on "an old 31-15 Singer head"; then 36-inch "yardage" machines, coming on "in about 42 or 43, about 44", strong enough to tuft heavy duck (12 to 22 ounce) into throw rugs. The "handmade candlewick bedspread" had "turned into a machine-tufted chenille spread"; chenille bathrobes and tufted sandals followed.
