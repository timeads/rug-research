A History of Tufted Rugs · by Tim Eads

The short version

The book on two sheets: what each chapter argues, and the dates that carry it. Every claim here is made, and sourced, in the chapter it links to.

In brief

Tufting puts yarn into a cloth that already exists, with a needle. Women did this by hand on New England bed rugs from at least 1778 and on candlewick spreads, and in Dalton, Georgia, a girl's bedspread of 1895 grew into a roadside trade and then a machine industry. The tufting machine came out of the sewing machine from 1920, gained many needles and pattern control, and was widened for broadloom carpet, which undercut the woven carpet trade. It left America as a machine to buy, reaching Japan and western Europe between 1954 and 1967 and China later; India's hand-tufting has no dated beginning yet. The hand gun has no single inventor, computers took over the pattern, and after 2018 the gun became a home craft and an artist's tool.

What tufting is

  1. Chapter 1 How rugs are made, and where tufting fits

    Six objects, from the Pazyryk carpet of the 5th to 4th century BCE to a multiple-needle patent of 1941, show two ways of making pile: with the cloth, on a loom, or into a cloth that is already finished, with a needle or a hook. Tufting belongs with the sewn and hooked rugs, not with the loom.

  2. Chapter 2 Before the machine: bed rugs and needle-made pile

    Bed rugs marked with women's names, from "Mary Foot AD 1778" onwards, were sewn with wool pile through a woven cloth, and candlewick spreads of the 1810s and 1820s carried the method on. The principle of the tufting machine was worked by hand in New England long before Dalton.

  3. Chapter 3 Brussels, Wilton and Kidderminster: woven carpet before tufting

    From the 1730s British and then American looms supplied carpet for every purse below knotted pile, from flat Kidderminster cloth to Wilton. That woven industry was the market tufted carpet later undercut, reportedly at about half its wholesale price.

Dalton, the machine and nylon

  1. Chapter 4 From candlewick spreads to the Bedspread Capital

    Near Dalton, Georgia, in 1895, fifteen-year-old Catherine Evans made a candlewick spread; her first sale came in 1900, for $2.50. Her craft became women's home piece-work, sold at the roadside and to department stores, then passed into machines, in factories and at home, and those machines were later widened for carpet.

  2. Chapter 5 Building the machine

    The tufting machine grew out of the sewing machine in Tennessee and Georgia from 1920, when William Barnett of Knoxville filed for a "Sewing Machine" that was in fact a tufting machine. Its needles multiplied (in patents from 1941), it gained pattern control in the 1950s and was widened to broadloom, and building it settled with a few firms in Chattanooga and Blackburn.

  3. Chapter 6 Nylon and the broadloom boom

    DuPont's earliest nylon carpets in its records were woven, some laid in its own hotel in Wilmington. In 1958, as tufted carpet sales passed 100 million square yards (84 million square metres), it made a nylon for carpet, and spent the next decade selling it to retailers and households.

Around the world

  1. Chapter 7 Imported from America: tufting goes abroad

    Tufting left the United States as a machine to buy: Japan by 1954, Britain by 1955, West Germany by about 1956, France in the mid-1950s and the Netherlands by 1967. No national record found so far names a rival inventor of the method.

  2. Chapter 8 China: palace carpets, export factories and Cuihuangkou

    China wove carpets for its palaces and exported them from Tianjin long before tufting arrived. Tufting came late and through imported machines, from the 1980s at the earliest on present evidence, yet Tianjin's carpet institute drafted the national standard for tufted carpet in 1989.

  3. Chapter 10 India: Bhadohi, Panipat and the hand-tufted carpet

    India's carpet belt now makes, teaches and exports hand-tufted carpet, and in 2025 a Bhadohi firm was reported to have made the largest hand-tufted carpet, for a mosque in Astana. No source found so far says when hand-tufting arrived.

The gun, the industry and the robot

  1. Chapter 9 From the turfing implement to the tufting gun

    The tufting gun has no single inventor. Hand tools like Ebenezer Ross's "Turfing Implement" of 1891 gained a motor in 1932, air in 1946, a trigger in 1952 and the name "tufting gun" by 1962; in the 1970s Hartmut Scholz in Germany patented an air-fed gun, and Tai Ping in Hong Kong, by one account, motorised its own.

  2. Chapter 11 The industry today: broadloom mills and custom rug houses

    Most of the firms traced here that tuft today came to it from another trade, whether dyeing, yarn, weaving or tapestry, and mergers have gathered mills and studios alike into fewer groups, inside which hand and machine work often sit side by side.

  3. Chapter 12 Programmable tufting: from the pattern attachment to the robot

    Pattern control moved along two lines: on the broadloom machine, from chain-driven yarn-feed rolls to computers and servo motors; and, from the 1990s, the single hand gun was carried over the backing by a computer-guided frame. In some studios that use such robots, people still finish the rugs by hand.

  4. Chapter 13 Hobby tufting, poke-poke and contemporary artists

    After 2018 the powered gun moved from workshops into homes, sold with instruction by supply firms such as the author's own, Tuft the World. By the Chinese press's account, social media carried the craze to mainland China as "poke-poke" by 2021, and artists took the gun into carved and sculptural work, books, videos and galleries.
