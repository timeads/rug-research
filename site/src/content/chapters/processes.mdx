In 1949 Sergei Rudenko's excavation of Barrow 5 in the Pazyryk valley, in the Altai Mountains, found a wool pile carpet frozen in ice, after looters had left the tomb open.[@process-knotted-pazyryk] Its pile is knotted: short lengths of yarn tied round pairs of warps, one row at a time, cut to height and locked with wefts. It is the oldest known knotted-pile carpet, and its date is still argued. Radiocarbon puts it in the 5th to 4th century BCE, while the Hermitage listing dates the barrow to 252–238 BCE.[@process-knotted-pazyryk]

Tufting makes pile too, by a different route. In weaving, pile and backing are made together; tufting adds pile yarn to a backing that already exists.[@process-tufting-overview] This chapter sorts the main ways of making a rug by that difference, so that a reader can tell tufting from the rest and see where it sits among them. The other twelve chapters tell the tufting story itself.

## Pile tied by hand

The knot varies. The symmetric knot, called Turkish or Ghiordes, passes round two neighbouring warps and brings both ends up between them; it is the knot of the Pazyryk carpet. The asymmetric knot, called Persian or Senneh, wraps fully round one warp and partly round the next, and is said to allow finer, more curving designs.[@process-knotted-knot-types] The Spanish knot is tied round a single warp, with alternate rows on alternate warps; the Textile Museum has an armorial carpet knotted this way and dated 1415–1458.[@process-knotted-spanish-knot]

According to Britannica, Tibetan weavers work differently: the yarn is looped under a warp, brought towards the weaver over a metal gauge rod and looped round the next warp; after each row a knife run along a groove in the rod cuts the loops into pile.[@process-knotted-tibetan] Loops first, cut afterwards: tufting, too, leaves loops that can then be cut.[@process-tufting-overview]

Spacing changes the fabric. The Finnish ryijy, or rya, has long knotted pile in widely spaced rows, with many ground picks between them, giving a shaggy, heavy cloth first used as a bedcover; the Victoria and Albert Museum has one marked with the initials AFBD and the date 1814.[@process-knotted-rya-vam] Knotting was also a European manufacture. A carpet attributed to Thomas Whitty of the Axminster carpet manufactory in Devon, and dated about 1765, is hand-knotted wool.[@met-928622]

## Rugs without pile: felt and flatweave

Felt is not woven at all. Britannica defines it as wool, fur or some hair fibres interlocked by heat, moisture and friction, with no adhesive, and says that non-woven felts are considered the first textile goods produced.[@process-felt-definition] The firmest early evidence found for felt floor and wall coverings comes from the Pazyryk burials: appliqué hangings, saddlecloths and cushions, of felt cut and sewn onto felt grounds.[@process-felt-definition; @process-felt-pazyryk-hangings]

<Figure id="process-felt-pazyryk-hangings" size="margin" caption="Fragment of felt appliqué for a carpet or wall hanging, showing a bird, from Pazyryk barrow no. 5." />

Felt rugs are still made. Kyrgyz shyrdak makers cut two felts of contrasting colour at once, swap the pieces and sew them onto a coarser felt base, a craft UNESCO placed on its Urgent Safeguarding List in 2012.[@process-felt-shyrdak]

Flatweaves have no pile. Kilims, Navajo blankets, dhurries and Swedish rölakan are all tapestry-woven: a weft-faced plain weave in which each coloured weft runs back and forth only where its colour is needed and is packed down to hide the warp. Britannica dates the earliest known tapestry to Egyptian linen of 1483–1411 BCE from the tomb of Thutmose IV; a 1923 account gives the tomb of Thutmose III and 1500–1400 BCE.[@process-flatweave-earliest-tapestry-egypt]

Where two colours meet in a kilim, the weaver can leave a slit, dovetail the two wefts round a shared warp, or interlock them; the Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco hold a 17th-century kilim in slit tapestry.[@process-flatweave-kilim-famsf]

<Figure id="process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn" caption="Cotton dhurrie from North India, late 19th century, 232 by 137 cm, with elephants and horses." />

In India the cotton dhurrie is beaten down with a panja, a hand tool with metal prongs, until the wefts cover the warp. The Penn Museum says that dhurries of the late 19th century were mostly made by prison labour.[@process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn] Navajo weavers work tapestry on an upright loom with no moving parts; from the 1880s traders such as J. L. Hubbell at Ganado steered them towards bordered floor rugs for Anglo buyers.[@process-flatweave-navajo]

## Pile made on the loom

In Europe pile moved onto the loom, the subject of chapter 3. In Brussels carpet, long steel wires are inserted between the linen and worsted warps, and the worsted loops up over them.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop] Wilton is the cut-pile form of Brussels, made on the same narrow looms.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop; @wilton-1741] Where Brussels carpet ran five or six coloured yarns in every pile line, Richard Whytock of Edinburgh printed the pattern onto a single pile warp before weaving. The Glasgow School of Art Archives say he patented his "Tapestry carpet loom" in 1832; another account dates it to about 1840, and the patent has not been found.[@process-loompile-tapestry-brussels]

James Templeton and William Quiglay, in Glasgow, patented an improved chenille method in July 1839. A "weft blanket" is woven in the pattern colours and cut into strips; each strip is folded into a furry chenille yarn, which is woven into a backing as the pile weft. The result is seamless cut pile without tying a knot.[@process-loompile-chenille-axminster] In 1876, according to Britannica's student encyclopedia, Halcyon Skinner perfected a power loom for Royal Axminster that made patterned cut-pile carpet cheaper, a claim still to be checked against a second source.[@skinner-axminster-1876]

<Figure id="process-loompile-chenille-axminster" size="margin" caption="Stair-carpet sample by James Templeton of Glasgow, late 19th century, its pile woven in chenille strips." />

A spool Axminster loom photographed at Shaw Industries in Dalton shows the principle. Chains overhead carry spools, each wound with one row of coloured pile yarn, and the loom takes one spool at a time, inserts a row of tufts, cuts them and weaves them in. The identification is from the photograph alone.[@own-spool-axminster-loom] In all of these, the loom makes the pile and its backing together.[@process-tufting-overview]

## Pile added to a finished cloth

Other rugs start from cloth that is already woven. In a yarn-sewn rug, yarn is sewn with a needle in a continuous running stitch through a closely woven cloth, leaving loops on the face that are usually clipped.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs]

<Figure id="process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs" caption="Yarn-sewn hearth rug, 1800–1820: cut and uncut wool loops on plain-woven linen, with a boy and girl under a grapevine." />

The Connecticut Historical Society's hearth rug of 1800–1820 shows a boy and girl under a grapevine, with roses in the corners. The Metropolitan Museum's bed rugs use the same needle-worked running stitch, "not hooked, as was once assumed".[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs; @met-13-207] Those bed rugs are the subject of chapter 2.

Shirred rugs sew strips of fabric onto the foundation. In chenille shirring a strip is stitched lengthwise down the middle and gathered into a caterpillar-like strand, which is sewn flat along the pattern. Historic New England's "Apple Tree Farm" rug, from Cogswell's Grant in Essex, Massachusetts, is dated 1820–1830.[@process-sewn-shirred-hne]

Hooking pulls strips of fabric or yarn up through a woven foundation, usually burlap, with a hook, making loops on the surface. Where it began is argued. W. W. Kent's *The Hooked Rug* (1930) traced it to Yorkshire, as Wikipedia reports; a Colonial Williamsburg label says it seems to have begun in New England and south-east Canada in the 1840s and spread once burlap arrived.[@process-hooking-origins; @process-hooking-cw-1983-611-1] The Canadian Museum of History says burlap was not sold commercially in North America until the 1850s, and that the oldest known Canadian hooked rug was hooked in 1860 by Abigail Smith, aged 13, at New Maryland, New Brunswick.[@process-hooking-origins]

Hooking could also be organised as outwork. The Grenfell Mission in northern Newfoundland and Labrador gave women kits of pre-dyed material and burlap stencilled with designs, then sold the mats to fund its medical work; sources date the start to 1906, 1909 or 1913.[@process-hooking-grenfell] Its "Polar Bear on Ice Floe" of about 1930, in the Baltimore Museum of Art, is hooked in wool and rayon or silk jersey on jute burlap; the Mission supplied used stockings donated by women in North American and British cities.[@process-hooking-grenfell-bma]

<PullQuote from="process-hooking-grenfell-bma" who="Grenfell Mission slogan, quoted by the Baltimore Museum of Art">When your stockings run, let them run to Labrador!</PullQuote>

Needle tools were patented too. Ebenezer Ross of Toledo, Ohio, patented a two-handled needle-and-gauge tool in 1891 as a "Turfing Implement" (chapter 9).[@pat-us463548a] Some strip rugs need no foundation cloth at all. Braided rugs coil a three-strand braid of cloth strips and sew it edge to edge into a round or oval mat; Historic New England's circular braided wool rug from Castle Tucker, Wiscasset, Maine, is dated 1860–1890, and no museum-dated braided rug earlier than that has yet been found.[@process-braided-hne] Crocheted rugs work strips into chained loops with a hook. The American Folk Art Museum's crocheted rug of 1880–1900 uses hand-dyed wool, woven wool cloth and recycled ingrain carpet.[@process-crochet-knit-afam]

## Tufting

Tufting belongs with the needle-made rugs. A needle, or a bar of hundreds of needles, pierces a primary backing and leaves loops, which can be cut. The tufts are then locked with latex or another adhesive, often laminated to a secondary backing. Unlike a loom, the tufting needle does not make the cloth it works in, and Britannica's student encyclopedia names weaving and tufting as the two main methods of making carpet today.[@process-tufting-overview]

<Figure id="pat-us2335487a" size="margin" caption="Joe C. Cobble, “Multiple Needle Tufting Machine”, US patent 2,335,487, filed 1941 and granted 1943." />

The tools were mechanised in the 20th century. Joe C. Cobble and George B. Muse's "Multiple Needle Tufting Machine" was filed in January 1941 and granted in November 1943.[@pat-us2335487a] According to the Carpet and Rug Institute, machinery developed for making chenille rugs was widened to make larger rugs and broadloom carpet (chapters 4 and 5).[@chenille-widened] By one count, still to be traced to its source, 10 per cent of American carpet was tufted in 1950.[@ttw-tufted-share] One textile source puts tufted products at more than 90 per cent of carpet now.[@process-tufting-overview]

## Other industrial methods

Industry has other ways to make a floor covering, some with no yarn at all. In needle-punching, barbed needles drag fibres through a loose web and tangle them into a felt-like fabric; one educational source says needled synthetics entered home furnishings in the late 1950s.[@process-industrial-needlepunch] A 2007 trade article credits the Dutch firm Heuga with the first carpet tile, a bitumen-backed needlefelt square, about fifty years earlier; British retailers say instead that Heuga was founded in 1956, or that the first commercial tile came in 1958.[@process-industrial-carpet-tile]

Flocked flooring shoots cut nylon fibres, charged in an electric field, end-first into wet adhesive so that they stand upright. BD Online and Forbo say the first was made by Flotex at Château-Renault in France in the 1960s; Low & Bonar launched it in Britain in November 1971.[@process-industrial-flocked] Fusion bonding sets pile yarn into a layer of adhesive, usually PVC; Encyclopedia.com says Milliken bought the American rights to the English process in July 1969, but when it was invented has not been found.[@process-industrial-fusion-bonded] Knitted carpet loops pile and backing yarns together in one operation, and no date for its introduction has been found.[@process-industrial-knitted]

Printing adds pattern after the carpet is made. Dye is forced through a stencilled screen, one for each colour, into plain carpet, usually tufted. The Austrian firm Zimmer dates its carpet printing on a flat-bed machine to 1962 and its large rotary-screen carpet printing to 1968. Later machines inject dye from computer-controlled jets, with no screens.[@process-industrial-printed]

This chapter argues that tufting belongs with the sewn and hooked rugs rather than with the loom. Like them, it puts pile into a cloth that already exists, and that separation of pile from backing is what sets it apart from knotting and weaving.
