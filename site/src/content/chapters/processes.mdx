The plainest way to see where tufting fits is to look closely at a handful of rugs and ask of each one where its pile came from. Some answer with a knot, some with the loom, and some with a needle and a cloth that was finished before the pile went in; a few have no pile at all. Tufting gives the needle's answer: it puts pile yarn into a backing that already exists, where a loom makes pile and backing together.[@process-tufting-overview] The six objects below are taken in that order, ending where the rest of this book begins.

## A carpet in the ice

In 1949 Sergei Rudenko's excavation of Barrow 5 in the Pazyryk valley, in the Altai Mountains, found a wool pile carpet frozen in ice. Looters had been there first and left the tomb open.[@process-knotted-pazyryk] The carpet measures 183 by 200 cm. Its field carries a cross-like motif, and deer and mounted horsemen ride round its borders.[@process-knotted-pazyryk]

Look closely and every tuft is a knot. The weaver tied short lengths of yarn round pairs of warps, one row at a time, cut them to height and locked each row with wefts; the knot used is the symmetric one, often called Turkish or Ghiordes.[@process-knotted-pazyryk] Its date is still argued, between the 5th to 4th century BCE given by radiocarbon and the 252–238 BCE at which the Hermitage listing dates the barrow, but no older knotted-pile carpet is known.[@process-knotted-pazyryk]

The knot did not stay the same. Weavers also use an asymmetric knot, called Persian or Senneh, which is said to allow finer, more curving designs;[@process-knotted-knot-types] in Spain they tied a knot round a single warp, as in an armorial carpet of 1415–1458 in the Textile Museum;[@process-knotted-spanish-knot] and in Finland they spaced long knots far apart to make the shaggy ryijy, or rya, first used on beds.[@process-knotted-rya-vam] According to Britannica, Tibetan weavers loop the yarn over a metal gauge rod and then cut the whole row of loops with a knife run along a groove in the rod.[@process-knotted-tibetan] That order, loops first and cut afterwards, is one tufting shares.[@process-tufting-overview]

The same barrow held floor and wall coverings with no pile at all: hangings, saddlecloths and cushions of felt, cut and sewn onto felt grounds.[@process-felt-pazyryk-hangings] Felt is wool, fur or some hair fibres locked together by heat, moisture and friction, and Britannica says that non-woven felts are considered the first textile goods produced.[@process-felt-definition] Kyrgyz women still make felt floor rugs called shyrdaks, cutting two felts of contrasting colour at once and swapping the pieces, a craft UNESCO placed on its Urgent Safeguarding List in 2012.[@process-felt-shyrdak]

## A dhurrie from North India

The second object has no pile either, but it is woven. The Penn Museum's cotton dhurrie from North India, made in the late 19th century, is 232 by 137 cm, with elephants and horses that the museum links to Mughal hunting carpets and Iranian medallion rugs.[@process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn] Dhurrie weavers beat the wefts down with a panja, a hand tool with metal prongs, until they cover the warp completely. The museum adds that dhurries of the time were mostly made by prison labour.[@process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn]

<Figure id="process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn" caption="Cotton dhurrie from North India, late 19th century, 232 by 137 cm, with elephants and horses." />

The dhurrie is a tapestry weave, like the kilim, the Navajo blanket and the Swedish rölakan: each coloured weft runs back and forth only where its colour is needed. Britannica traces the technique to Egyptian linen of 1483–1411 BCE from the tomb of Thutmose IV; a 1923 account gives the tomb of Thutmose III and 1500–1400 BCE.[@process-flatweave-earliest-tapestry-egypt] Where two colours meet, a kilim weaver can leave a slit, dovetail the wefts round a shared warp, or interlock them.[@process-flatweave-kilim-famsf] Navajo weavers work tapestry on an upright loom with no moving parts, and from the 1880s traders such as J. L. Hubbell at Ganado steered them towards bordered floor rugs for Anglo buyers.[@process-flatweave-navajo]

## A carpet from Devon, and a sample from Glasgow

<Figure id="met-928622" size="wide" caption="Carpet attributed to Thomas Whitty, Axminster carpet manufactory, Devon, about 1765: wool, knotted pile." />

Around 1765 the Axminster carpet manufactory in Devon made a hand-knotted wool carpet that the Metropolitan Museum attributes to Thomas Whitty. It measures 17 feet by almost 15, and the Met acquired it in 2025.[@met-928622] It was made in a European workshop, but every tuft in it is a knot, as at Pazyryk.[@met-928622; @process-knotted-pazyryk]

Europe's carpet makers were already learning to make the loom form the pile instead.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop] In Brussels carpet, long steel wires are laid between the warps and the worsted pile yarn loops up over them; Wilton is the same carpet with the loops cut.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop; @wilton-1741] Colour remained the difficulty, since a Brussels loom ran five or six coloured yarns in every pile line. Richard Whytock of Edinburgh printed the pattern onto one pile warp before weaving; the Glasgow School of Art Archives say he patented his "Tapestry carpet loom" in 1832, though another account dates it to about 1840 and the patent has not been found.[@process-loompile-tapestry-brussels]

<Figure id="process-loompile-chenille-axminster" size="margin" caption="Stair-carpet sample by James Templeton of Glasgow, late 19th century, its pile woven in chenille strips." />

The Victoria and Albert Museum keeps a stair-carpet sample by James Templeton of Glasgow, from the late 19th century, with a Persian palmette design. Its pile is not knotted at all.[@process-loompile-chenille-axminster] Templeton and William Quiglay patented an improved chenille method in July 1839: a "weft blanket" is woven in the pattern colours and cut into strips, each strip is folded into a furry chenille yarn, and that yarn is woven into a backing as the pile.[@process-loompile-chenille-axminster] In 1876, according to Britannica's student encyclopedia, Halcyon Skinner perfected a power loom for Royal Axminster that made patterned cut pile cheaper, a claim still to be checked against a second source.[@skinner-axminster-1876] Different as they are, the Whitty carpet and the Templeton sample share one thing: the pile and the backing were made together.[@process-tufting-overview]

## A hearth rug from Connecticut

The fourth object reverses that. A hearth rug in the Connecticut Historical Society, made between 1800 and 1820, has an oval medallion with a boy and a girl under a grapevine, and roses in the corners. Family tradition links it to the Samuel Hanmer house in Wethersfield.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs] Its pile was not woven in. The maker took a plain-woven linen cloth and sewed wool through it with a needle in a continuous running stitch, leaving loops on the face, some cut and some not.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs]

<Figure id="process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs" caption="Yarn-sewn hearth rug, 1800–1820: cut and uncut wool loops on plain-woven linen, with a boy and girl under a grapevine." />

The Metropolitan Museum's bed rugs are worked the same way, in a needle-worked running stitch "not hooked, as was once assumed"; they are the subject of chapter 2.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs; @met-13-207] Others sewed on cloth instead of yarn. In a chenille-shirred rug, a fabric strip is stitched down the middle, gathered into a caterpillar-like strand and sewn flat along the pattern, as in Historic New England's "Apple Tree Farm" rug of 1820–1830 from Cogswell's Grant in Essex, Massachusetts.[@process-sewn-shirred-hne]

## Two sisters in New Brunswick

In 1860, at New Maryland near Fredericton, New Brunswick, Susanna Smith, aged sixteen, designed a rug and her sister Abigail, aged thirteen, hooked it. The Canadian Museum of History calls it the oldest known Canadian hooked rug.[@process-hooking-origins] A hooker pulls strips of fabric or yarn up through a woven foundation with a hook, making loops on the surface. The usual foundation was burlap, which the same museum says was not sold commercially in North America until the 1850s.[@process-hooking-origins]

Where hooking began is argued. W. W. Kent's *The Hooked Rug* (1930) traced it to Yorkshire, as Wikipedia reports, while a Colonial Williamsburg label says it seems to have begun in New England and south-east Canada in the 1840s and spread once burlap arrived.[@process-hooking-origins; @process-hooking-cw-1983-611-1]

By the early 20th century, hooking could also be organised as charitable outwork. The Grenfell Mission in northern Newfoundland and Labrador gave women kits of pre-dyed material and burlap stencilled with designs, and sold the mats to fund its medical work; sources date the start to 1906, 1909 or 1913.[@process-hooking-grenfell] Its "Polar Bear on Ice Floe", of about 1930, now in the Baltimore Museum of Art, is hooked in wool and rayon or silk jersey on jute burlap. The Mission supplied used stockings donated by women in North American and British cities.[@process-hooking-grenfell-bma]

<PullQuote from="process-hooking-grenfell-bma" who="Grenfell Mission slogan, quoted by the Baltimore Museum of Art">When your stockings run, let them run to Labrador!</PullQuote>

Some strip rugs needed no foundation cloth at all. Braided rugs coil a three-strand braid of cloth strips and sew it edge to edge into a round or oval mat,[@process-braided-hne] and crocheted rugs work strips into chained loops with a hook; the American Folk Art Museum has one of 1880–1900 made partly from recycled ingrain carpet.[@process-crochet-knit-afam]

## A patent drawing, 1941

<Figure id="pat-us2335487a" size="margin" caption="Joe C. Cobble, “Multiple Needle Tufting Machine”, US patent 2,335,487, filed 1941 and granted 1943." />

The last object is a drawing. In January 1941 Joe C. Cobble and George B. Muse filed for a "Multiple Needle Tufting Machine", granted in November 1943.[@pat-us2335487a] Fifty years earlier Ebenezer Ross of Toledo, Ohio, had patented a two-handled needle-and-gauge tool as a "Turfing Implement" (chapter 9).[@pat-us463548a] Both work with needles, as the maker of the Connecticut hearth rug did.

In tufting, a needle, or a bar of hundreds of needles, pierces a primary backing and leaves loops, which can be cut. The tufts are then locked with latex or another adhesive, often with a secondary backing laminated on. Unlike a loom, the tufting needle does not make the cloth it works in.[@process-tufting-overview] According to the Carpet and Rug Institute, machinery developed for chenille rugs was widened to make larger rugs and broadloom carpet (chapters 4 and 5).[@chenille-widened] By one count, still to be traced to its source, 10 per cent of American carpet was tufted in 1950;[@ttw-tufted-share] one textile source puts tufted products at more than 90 per cent of carpet now.[@process-tufting-overview]

Industry has since found ways to make a floor covering with no yarn construction at all: barbed needles that tangle a loose web of fibres into a felt-like fabric,[@process-industrial-needlepunch] nylon fibres shot upright into wet adhesive in an electric field,[@process-industrial-flocked] and pile yarn set into a layer of PVC.[@process-industrial-fusion-bonded] Britannica's student encyclopedia still names weaving and tufting as the two main methods of making carpet.[@process-tufting-overview]

This chapter argues that tufting belongs with the sewn and hooked rugs rather than with the loom. The Pazyryk carpet, the Whitty carpet and the Templeton sample were made with their pile; the Connecticut hearth rug, the Smith sisters' rug and the tufting machine all start from a cloth that already exists and put the pile into it.[@process-tufting-overview]
