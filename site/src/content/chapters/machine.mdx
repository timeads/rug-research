In 1920 William H. Barnett of Knoxville, Tennessee, applied for a patent on a "Sewing Machine". It was granted on 24 April 1923, and its text says the machine "may be more specifically termed a tufting machine".[@pat-us1452632a] Two years later, on 21 July 1922, August J. Carter of Chattanooga filed for a "Tufting-Attachment Mechanism for Sewing Machines", granted on 26 February 1924. It put a needle, a loop former and a separate cutting knife on a sewing machine.[@pat-us1485213a] It is the first patent in this database to combine the three, and with Barnett's it shows that the machine line starts from the sewing machine, not from the old hand tools.[@ttw-carter-1922]

Chapter 4 told how machines came to Dalton's bedspread trade in the 1930s. This chapter starts earlier and goes on later. It corrects one common line on the way: the claim that the first tufting machine was built in 1930 has no patent behind it.[@ttw-first-machine-1930-refuted] The patents show work going back to 1920.

## Sewing-machine makers and rug mills, 1920–1936

Carter's firm went on patenting. John M. Gladish filed a "Tufting Attachment Mechanism for Sewing Machines" on 20 September 1927, assigned to Carter Brothers Inc. of Chattanooga,[@pat-us1800522a] and Carter himself patented a "Tufted Rug", filed in 1933 and also assigned to Carter Brothers: a patent for the product, not the machine.[@pat-us2042503a]

His next three patents, a "Loop and Pile Forming Machine" (filed 7 July 1928), a "Tufting Machine Looper and Cutter" (filed 2 October 1929) and a "Tufting Machine" (filed 21 January 1930), were assigned to a rug mill, Valway Rug Mills of LaGrange, Georgia, and granted in May 1933.[@pat-us1907292a; @pat-us1909530a; @pat-us1909531a] A fifth, filed in 1934 from Chattanooga, was assigned half to Robert Blair Cannon.[@pat-us2224866a] Another "Tufting machine", granted in 1933 to L. A. Smith, also comes from LaGrange and was assigned to Truline.[@pat-us1903946a]

<Figure id="pat-us1907292a" size="margin" caption="John M. Gladish, “Loop and Pile Forming Machine”, filed 7 July 1928 for Valway Rug Mills, LaGrange, sheet 1." />

The sewing-machine industry took an interest too. The "Rug Tufting Machine" of Harry E. Dykeman of Chicago, filed on 30 November 1928, was assigned to the sewing-machine maker Union Special Machine Co.[@pat-us1831485a] Walter Loos patented a "Machine for Making Rugs, Carpets, and the Like" (filed 1932) and a "Tufting Machine" (filed 1935).[@pat-us1970703a; @pat-us2070287a] Further patents titled "Tufting machine" were granted to Maples and Weaver of Scottsboro, Alabama, in 1934 and to Warren in 1936.[@pat-us1963441a; @pat-us2058047a]

One machine of these years has its own monument. The carpet tufting apparatus of Ernest J. Moench, dated 1928 and now displayed at Shaw Industries in Dalton, was a single-needle machine that punched loops of thick yarn through a backing; Moench patented a multi-needle version, which was cited in improvement patents as late as 1980. The American Society of Mechanical Engineers named it Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark no. 266 on 31 July 2018 and credits Moench with the first machine, where Dalton accounts credit Looper. Moench's patent number has not been found, and ASME sources give both a "1928 patent" and one "issued 1934".[@tool-moench-1928]

Most of these patents came from Chattanooga and LaGrange, not Dalton. In this database, Dalton's own patents begin in the 1930s: Lawrence P. Smith's handheld "Motor Operated Tufting Machine" for Cabin Crafts, filed in 1932 (chapter 9),[@pat-us1917162a] then Glenn Looper's "Tufting Machine" of 1936 and Jess Kile's of 1939 (chapter 4).[@pat-us2123114a; @pat-us2389914a] Roy H. Windham of Rome, Georgia, shared a "Tufting machine" patent granted in 1938, assigned to Polly Prentiss, and filed a "Tufting Machine and Method" on 6 October 1939, assigned to Burl J. Bandy of Dalton.[@pat-us2112512a; @pat-us2311802a] Windham also wrote *The Tufting Machine: A Practical Text of Instruction for Machine Fixers*, published at Rome by the Southern Craft Company and in its third edition by 1941. No copy has been found on the Internet Archive or Open Library.[@book-windham-tufting-machine-1941]

## Many needles

Chapter 4 records the joint patent of Joe C. Cobble and George B. Muse. Its title names the change: "Multiple Needle Tufting Machine". It was filed on 24 January 1941 and granted on 30 November 1943, and it gives Chattanooga, Tennessee, and Sugar Valley, Georgia.[@pat-us2335487a] It is credited locally as the basis of modern tufting machines.[@co-cobble]

Other multi-needle patents followed. Lyman Hamrick of Gaffney, South Carolina, filed a "Multiple Needle Tufting Machine" in July 1944, granted in 1946,[@pat-us2411267a] and Walter W. Hamby of Dalton filed one of the same name on 20 May 1949, with a fifth share assigned to Isaac C. Adams.[@pat-us2595355a] Patents titled simply "Tufting machine" went to Bills and Patey of Dalton in 1944 and to C. M. Jones of Calhoun, Georgia, in 1954.[@pat-us2346467a; @pat-us2679218a] At Cobble Brothers in Chattanooga, James A. Cobble Sr. and Jr. filed a "Tufting Machine" on 4 March 1958, granted in 1961.[@patent-us2977905]

<Figure id="patent-us2977905" size="margin" caption="James A. Cobble Sr. and Jr., “Tufting Machine”, US patent 2,977,905, filed 4 March 1958, sheet 1." />

According to the Carpet and Rug Institute, the machinery made for chenille was widened, first for larger rugs and then for broadloom carpet.[@chenille-widened] Which machine first tufted at broadloom width, and when, is still to be found. A photograph at the Bandy Heritage Center is described as showing a Cobble Brothers loop-pile machine 216 inches wide, but it is undated.[@finding-cnc-mechanical-pattern-attachments] By one count, still to be traced to its source, 10 per cent of American carpet was tufted in 1950 and more than 90 per cent is today;[@ttw-tufted-share] another source puts tufted products at more than 90 per cent of carpet.[@process-tufting-overview] William A. Reynolds's *Innovation in the United States Carpet Industry, 1947–1963* (1968) covers these years.[@book-reynolds-innovation-1968]

## Telling the needles what to do

With many needles came patents for varying what they did. Joseph L. Card filed a "Thread Feed Mechanism and Pattern Control" on 16 May 1955, granted in 1958,[@pat-us2862465a] and an "Apparatus for Making Patterned Tufted Pile Fabric" on 24 December 1956, assigned to Cobble Brothers Machinery Co. of Chattanooga and granted on 3 May 1960.[@pat-us2935037a] A British filing of the same apparatus was made in 1957, though it has so far been identified only from search results.[@pat-gb853746a] Card's "Loop Shedder", filed in 1958, was assigned to Singer-Cobble Inc.[@pat-us2975736a] How pattern control grew from these mechanical devices into computer control is the subject of chapter 12.

## The machine builders

The machines were built by a few firms, mostly around Chattanooga, and their histories are known so far mainly from their own accounts and from search summaries still to be checked. According to Card-Monroe's history, the Cobble brothers began making tufting equipment for chenille bedspreads in the 1930s.[@co-cobble; @co-card-monroe] Grace's Guide says Cobble Brothers Machinery opened a British subsidiary in 1955, and that by 1960 the American parent had been taken over by Singer, making Singer-Cobble, which also absorbed British Tufting Machinery of Blackburn. Spencer H. Wright led Cobble for more than thirty years, with plants in Blackburn and Dalton, and the Belgian Vandewiele group bought the firm in 2013.[@co-cobble] A Cotton Town caption says instead that Cobble took over Albert Mill in 1956, so the start of the British plant is still to be settled.[@pat-gb853746a]

The British arm also filed its own inventions. A British priority filing of 9 July 1960, so far identified only from search results, underlies a Singer-Cobble needle-plate patent,[@pat-gb880860a] and Ernest Kenneth Lund of Singer-Cobble Ltd, Blackburn, filed "Improvements in or relating to tufting machines" on 19 March 1965, published in 1967.[@pat-gb1067502a] Edgar Pickering left Singer-Cobble to found his own Blackburn firm in 1964, and Ronald Ellison founded Ellison Tufting Machinery in 1961.[@pat-gb1067502a]

The Card family carried the trade on in Chattanooga. Tuftco says it was founded in 1960 as Southern Machine Company to build and sell tufting machines, and sold them in Europe, Asia and South America in the 1960s; an obituary of Lewis Card Sr. says instead that he and his brother Roy, after working at Cobble, founded Tuftco "in the late 1950s".[@co-tuftco] Card-Monroe says that Roy and Lewis Card Sr., the Cobbles' nephews, started at Cobble in the 1940s (the obituary says Lewis joined in 1939). The Charles Monroe Company was founded in 1981 by Charles Monroe and Lewis Card Jr., and became Card-Monroe in 1983 when Lewis and Roy Card invested. By the company's own figures it has built more than 2,500 machines, in use in 38 countries.[@co-card-monroe]

Many of the needles now come from Germany. Groz-Beckert of Ebingen made only knitting needles until 1980; in 1998 it bought the tufting-needle business of Josef Zimmermann of Aachen, sold under the brand "Eisbär", and then added tufting knives, loopers and reed fingers by acquisition up to 2004. It is now a major supplier of needles and parts for tufting machines.[@co-groz-beckert] German Wikipedia credits the machine itself to the United States and Britain, and no German builder of industrial broadloom tufting machines has been found,[@ner-de-no-machines] though Girmes-Werke of Oedt, near Krefeld, appears to have patented a "Plush tufting machine" about 1971, a record seen only in one search snippet.[@pat-de1785451c] How the machines went abroad is told in chapter 7.

By the industry's own figure, still to be traced to its source, more than 70 per cent of the world's carpet and about 85 per cent of America's is made within about an hour of Dalton.[@ttw-dalton-share] Geographers have studied why: John R. McGregor and Robert H. Maxey on "The Dalton, Georgia, Tufted Textile Concentration" in 1974, and Billie J. Walters and James O. Wheeler on "Localization Economies in the American Carpet Industry" in 1984.[@article-mcgregor-maxey-1974; @article-walters-wheeler-1984]

This chapter argues that the tufting machine began as an attachment for the sewing machine, patented in Tennessee and Georgia from 1920, more than a decade before Looper's Dalton patent, and that once its needles were multiplied in the 1940s and the machine was widened to broadloom, its building settled with a few firms in Chattanooga and Blackburn.
