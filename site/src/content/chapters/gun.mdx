A cardboard box in the Tuft the World collection holds a rug tool with two wooden handles that slide against each other: a steel needle in one, a gauge in the other. The box names its maker, The Holley Associates of Torrington, Connecticut. The printed instructions inside describe the action: insert the needle, then "push down the gauge to hold the loop while the needle is withdrawn", and so on, "alternately inserting the needle and the gauge".[@ttw-susan-burr] The box carries a poem.

<PullQuote from="ttw-susan-burr" who="Box of Ye “Susan Burr” Hooked Rug Machine, The Holley Associates, Torrington">Susan Burr, in the days gone by, / Fashioned rugs such as you or I / With the self-same needle / In the self-same way.</PullQuote>

No record of a real Susan Burr has been found.[@ttw-susan-burr] This chapter follows its two-part action, a needle and a second part that holds or cuts the loop, from a patent of 1891 to the powered tufting gun.

## Ross's "Turfing Implement"

On 17 November 1891 Ebenezer Ross of Toledo, Ohio, was granted a patent for a "Turfing Implement"; "turfing" is the patent's own word. He had filed it that July as an improvement on his own "Embroidery Machine", patented in 1881 while he lived at Wauseon, Ohio.[@pat-us463548a; @pat-us251381a] The drawing shows a two-handled needle-and-gauge tool of the same form as the Susan Burr.[@pat-us463548a]

  <Figure id="ttw-susan-burr" image={1} caption="“How to Use Ye Susan Burr Hooked Rug Machine”: the printed instructions, with a diagram of the needle, ring and gauge." />
</Pair>

Ross sold patterns as well as tools. The Winterthur Library holds his firm's catalogue of rug patterns, E. Ross & Co., Toledo, 1891. Most of its patterns were carried over from earlier catalogues, but two of them were his own, "Mastiff Dog" and "Lion and Palm", which Montgomery Ward and Sears later copied.[@wint-lib-ross-1891] A hooked rug made in Toledo between 1890 and 1900 from the "Lion and Palm" pattern is at the Art Institute of Chicago.[@aic-71710] A Christie's lot note also credits Ross with a mechanical punch hook in 1886.[@ross-toledo]

Tools of this kind travelled. The Auckland War Memorial Museum holds a "Semi-automated 'Jewel' rug-hooking tool for punching pile through backing", painted "Jewel Pat. Allowed", which its record says was patented in the United States in 1886. The donor picked it up in Auckland in 1930.[@wc-83070426]

Ross's patent expired in 1908.[@pat-us463548a] In August of that year Samuel Henry Perrier of Troy, Oregon, was granted a patent for a "Turfing Needle", for "tufted or pile work in embroidery and rug making".[@pat-us897200a]

## Needles for the home

The Susan Burr is one of the tools that followed. Its record dates it to the 1920s or 1930s, and a comparable machine at the Greer Heritage Museum (acc. 2002.1.1&2) is dated to the 1920s.[@ttw-susan-burr; @ttw-greer-susan-burr]

<Figure id="ttw-shuttle-needle" size="margin" caption="The All Steel Shuttle Needle of the Molter-Reinhard Co., Chicago: needle point in one handle, looper in the other." />

A second tool in the same collection, the All Steel Shuttle Needle of the Molter-Reinhard Co. of Chicago, is made wholly of steel. One handle carries the needle point and the other a "blue steel looper", and the printed directions describe the same alternation: "insert needle point as far as it will go, then insert looper (which is the other handle)". The firm sold yarn and patterns too, as Ross had done ("You will be pleased with our rug patterns"). The package is undated.[@ttw-shuttle-needle]

Other inventors patented hand tools of their own. John E. Garrett of New Glasgow, Nova Scotia, patented a "Rug Hooker" in 1926 and a second in 1933; the first is very likely the Bluenose Rug Hooker.[@pat-us1605711a; @pat-us1941346a] A Bucilla catalogue photographed at the Winterthur Library sold the Bluenose as "the fastest rug making innovation procurable today".[@wint-photo-bucilla-tools] E. J. Gearhart filed for a "Semiautomatic Turfing Device" in 1931, a spring-loaded punch needle by a later patent's account.[@pat-us1912977a; @pat-us3968757a] Foster K. Solliday of Grand Rapids, Michigan, filed for a "Hooked Rug Making Device" in 1935.[@pat-us2077719a]

## A motor, then air, then a trigger

The tufting machine, meanwhile, started from the sewing machine rather than the hand tool (chapter 5). In 1922 August J. Carter of Chattanooga filed for a "Tufting-Attachment Mechanism for Sewing Machines".[@ttw-carter-1922] Ten years later a hand tool with its own motor was patented in Dalton. Lawrence P. Smith of Dalton filed for a "Motor Operated Tufting Machine" on 23 June 1932, assigned to Cabin Crafts and granted on 4 July 1933: a handheld tool with the motor in its body and the needle below, the earliest motorised hand tufting tool found in this research.[@pat-us1917162a]

<Figure id="pat-us1917162a" size="margin" caption="Lawrence P. Smith, “Motor Operated Tufting Machine,” US patent 1,917,162, filed 1932 and assigned to Cabin Crafts." />

Air came after the war, in two patents filed in June and July 1946. Samuel P. Parker's "Tufting Device", assigned to Callaway Mills of LaGrange, Georgia, was an air-fed handheld tool.[@pat-us2422126a] James H. Shay's "Hooked Rug Machine", assigned to Shaycraft Inc. of Newtown Square, Pennsylvania, drove a hand rug tool with compressed-air pistons, and it cites Ross's patent of 1891.[@pat-us2442906a]

Next came a hand tool for cut pile. Melvin S. Hurley of Bridgend, England, filed in the United States in 1949 for a "Hand Tool for Making Rugs, Carpets and the Like with a Cut Pile", and a British patent of his for the same tool, so far seen only through search summaries, was published in 1951.[@pat-us2605727a; @pat-gb663196a] In October 1952 Hans G. Lustig of Lake Placid, New York, filed for a "Carpet Forming Device", a handheld tool with a trigger and a removable knife.[@pat-us2753820a]

## The "tufting gun" gets its name

Through the 1950s hand tools of this kind were patented as tufting machines or implements: by Theron F. Gifford of Chattanooga for the Ten-Tex Corp. (filed 1953), by Russell L. Sterner of Downey, California (1955), and by M. L. Ward and Lewis Teal for Callaway Mills (1956).[@pat-us2837045a; @pat-us2887076a; @pat-us2879731a] Cabin Crafts came back in 1962 with Charles D. Elliott's "Portable Machine for Tufting Cut Pile", "Hand carried and hand guided",[@pat-us3144844a] and Bigelow-Sanford was assigned O. J. Roberts's "Portable Tufting Machine", filed in 1964.[@pat-us3229653a]

The word "gun" appears in the patent of Alfred Schauer of Elmhurst, New York, filed on 25 June 1962 and granted in 1964. Schauer called his tool a "hand gun-type device", used the phrase "tufting gun", and said that such hand guns "have been used for some time". It is the earliest use of the term found so far, and Schauer's remark places the guns themselves earlier still.[@pat-us3142276a; @ttw-tufting-gun-term] Helmut C. Mueller's patent for Bigelow-Sanford, filed in 1965, speaks of a "handheld tufting gun".[@pat-us3389667a; @ttw-tufting-gun-term]

<Figure id="pat-us3142276a" caption="Alfred Schauer, “Device for Forming Cut Pile Fabrics and the Like,” US patent 3,142,276, filed 1962: the patent that uses the phrase “tufting gun”." />

Who used these guns, and for what, is less clear. Several of the patents went to companies, among them Cabin Crafts, Callaway Mills, Ten-Tex and Bigelow-Sanford. The custom studios (chapter 11) made claims of their own: trade sources say Stanislav V'Soske, whose workshop was in Grand Rapids, Michigan, developed "the modern hand-tufting technique" in 1924,[@co-vsoske] and Edward Fields of New York says its founder developed a hand-tufting tool, the "Magic Needle", in the mid-1930s.[@co-edward-fields] Neither claim has yet been matched to a patent or a dated tool.

One use is named in a patent. In 1990 William H. Satterfield filed for a "Hand-held Tufting Mending Gun", a Dalton patent assigned to Spencer Wright Industries. Its list of prior art cites the hand guns of Lustig, Gifford, Ward, Sterner, Schauer, Elliott and Roberts.[@pat-us5090341a] Shaw Industries' design team has told Tim Eads, in conversation, that hand-held guns are still used on the broadloom line to fix mistakes found at final inspection; a written source for this is still to be found.[@finding-shaw-mending-gun]

## Allersberg: the pneumatic gun

Europe had its own hand devices: a Danish "Apparatus for Hand Sewing Carpets" of 1938, known so far only through search summaries, has family members in five other countries, though whether its "sewing" means making pile is still to be confirmed.[@pat-ch214147a] In Germany a company grew up around the hand-tufting gun. According to the history page of Hofmann Handtuft-Technik GmbH, now in Nördlingen, trials with the hand-tuft technique began in 1969, and Scholz Handtuft-Technik KG was founded in Allersberg near Nuremberg in 1973, with Hartmut Scholz and Heinz Hofmann as managing directors. Scholz left in 1979, and the firm took Hofmann's name. Company catalogue material dates the first machines to 1973 and their market launch to 1975.[@co-hofmann-handtuft]

Scholz filed in Germany for a "Device for tufting carpets by hand" on 13 November 1973.[@pat-de2356585c2] Its American counterpart, granted on 13 July 1976, describes a pneumatic hand gun: a hollow needle pierces the backing, the yarn is carried through it in an air stream, and one synchronous drive works both the cutter and the needle lift. It cites the American hand-gun patents of Schauer, Elliott and Mueller.[@pat-us3968758a]

  <Figure id="pat-us3968758a" caption="Hartmut Scholz, “Arrangement for the Tufting of Carpets,” US patent 3,968,758, 1976: a pneumatic hand gun with an air-fed hollow needle." />
  <Figure id="pat-us4029031a" caption="Hartmut Scholz, “Device for Providing Rugs and the Like with Tufts,” US patent 4,029,031, 1977: a manual two-arm tool, fed from a yarn cone." />
</Pair>

Scholz's second patent family, with a German priority of 14 March 1975, went back to the hand tool. Its American member, granted in 1977, is a manual device with two arms worked alternately against the backing. One arm carries a fixed needle that guides the yarn; the other carries a blade that slides along the needle and cuts the yarn after each loop, closed by a spring. It cites Hurley's cut-pile hand tool.[@pat-us4029031a] This is Ross's two-part, alternate-stroke action with a blade added.

A second German gun is linked to Hans Hartleb, whose "hand tufting device" patent of 1978 has so far been seen only through search summaries.[@pat-de2815801a1] An Australian firm's patent, filed in 2008, describes "a traditional hand tufting gun for instance, Hofmann™ or Hartleb™, each of which are limited to tufting along an outline at a constant pile height". Its own aim, to put the gun on a computer-guided machine, belongs to chapter 12.[@pat-ep2077348b1]

## Hong Kong, and the tools that stayed by hand

By a Hong Kong account, a gun was also being motorised there in 1973, the year Scholz filed. Tai Ping Carpets, founded in 1956, had moved from hand-knotting to hand-tufting, by its own account.[@co-tai-ping] Its general manager, the engineer Yip Yuen-cheung (Anthony Yeh), developed a new cut-pile tufting needle, drawing, says the Hong Kong Heritage Project's Chinese text, on the American method of making carpet with a single-needle sewing machine and on household tufting tools (家用簇絨工具).[@ner-hk-taiping-needle] The same source says that in 1973 Tai Ping fitted its tufting gun with a motor and made it lighter.[@ner-hk-taiping-1973] The dates conflict. A 2021 trade article credits Yeh with a pneumatic gun in 1960, and Hong Kong heritage articles call the 1973 gun "the world's first" (全球首枝), a claim the American patents above contradict.[@ner-hk-taiping-1973; @ttw-conflict-gun-origin]

In mainland China (chapter 8) a utility model for "A hand-held horizontal electric weaving needle for weaving carpets" was filed in April 2004, and a second, filed that November, describes an electric gun with a small motor, a gear-transmission box and a connecting rod.[@pat-cn2723478y; @pat-cn2806530y]

The unpowered tool never disappeared. Manual speed-tufting tools with the same two-part alternating action are sold new in Mirzapur, India, though commercial pile work uses 220-volt electric guns.[@ttw-mirzapur-manual] Nor did the home tool. On 13 July 1976 the patent for Scholz's pneumatic gun was granted as US 3,968,758; the number before it went the same day to John S. Doyel's "Rug-tufting device suitable for home use by hobyists", a hand-operated tool "made primarily of molded plastic components".[@pat-us3968758a; @pat-us3968757a] Doyel's patent is very likely the one behind Ronco's 1975 "Speed-Tufting Kit", sold on television.[@ttw-ronco-speed-tufting-kit] That kit, and the home tool's later return, belong to chapter 13.

This chapter argues that the tufting gun has no single inventor. It grew out of hand tools like Ross's, a needle with a second part to hold or cut the loop. In the patents found so far, American hand tools gained a motor in 1932, air in 1946, a trigger in 1952 and the name "tufting gun" by 1962; in the 1970s Scholz in Germany patented an air-fed gun on the principle of today's professional guns, and Tai Ping in Hong Kong, by the Hong Kong Heritage Project's account, fitted its gun with a motor.
