A cardboard box in the Tuft the World collection, which belongs to the firm co-founded by this book's author,[@ttw-susan-burr; @co-tuft-the-world] holds a rug tool with two wooden handles that slide against each other: a steel needle in one, a gauge in the other. The box names its maker, The Holley Associates of Torrington, Connecticut, and the printed instructions inside describe the action: insert the needle, then "push down the gauge to hold the loop while the needle is withdrawn", and so on, "alternately inserting the needle and the gauge".[@ttw-susan-burr] On the box is a poem.

<PullQuote from="ttw-susan-burr" who="Box of Ye “Susan Burr” Hooked Rug Machine, The Holley Associates, Torrington">Susan Burr, in the days gone by, / Fashioned rugs such as you or I / With the self-same needle / In the self-same way.</PullQuote>

No record of a real Susan Burr has been found.[@ttw-susan-burr] The tool's two working parts, a needle and a second piece that holds or cuts the loop, run through this chapter, from a patent drawing of 1891 to the powered tufting gun.

## Ebenezer Ross's patent drawing, 1891

On 17 November 1891 Ebenezer Ross of Toledo, Ohio, was granted a patent for a "Turfing Implement"; "turfing" is the patent's own word. Its drawing shows a two-handled needle-and-gauge tool of the same form as the Susan Burr.[@pat-us463548a] Ross had filed it that July as an improvement on his own "Embroidery Machine", patented in 1881 while he lived at Wauseon, Ohio.[@pat-us463548a; @pat-us251381a]

<Pair>
  <Figure id="pat-us463548a" caption="Ebenezer Ross, “Turfing Implement,” US patent 463,548, granted 17 November 1891: a two-handled needle-and-gauge tool." />
  <Figure id="ttw-susan-burr" image={1} caption="“How to Use Ye Susan Burr Hooked Rug Machine”: the printed instructions, with a diagram of the needle, ring and gauge." />
</Pair>

Ross sold patterns as well as tools. The Winterthur Library holds his firm's catalogue of rug patterns, E. Ross & Co., Toledo, 1891. Most of its patterns were carried over from earlier catalogues, but two were his own, "Mastiff Dog" and "Lion and Palm", which Montgomery Ward and Sears later copied.[@wint-lib-ross-1891] A hooked rug made in Toledo between 1890 and 1900 from the "Lion and Palm" pattern is at the Art Institute of Chicago.[@aic-71710] A Christie's lot note also credits Ross with a mechanical punch hook in 1886.[@ross-toledo]

Tools of this kind travelled. The Auckland War Memorial Museum holds a "Semi-automated 'Jewel' rug-hooking tool for punching pile through backing", painted "Jewel Pat. Allowed", which its record says was patented in the United States in 1886. The donor picked it up in Auckland in 1930.[@wc-83070426]

Ross's patent expired in 1908.[@pat-us463548a] In August of that year Samuel Henry Perrier of Troy, Oregon, was granted a patent for a "Turfing Needle", for "tufted or pile work in embroidery and rug making".[@pat-us897200a] The Susan Burr was one of the two-handled tools that came after.

## A shuttle needle from Chicago

<Figure id="ttw-shuttle-needle" size="margin" caption="The All Steel Shuttle Needle of the Molter-Reinhard Co., Chicago: needle point in one handle, looper in the other." />

The Susan Burr's record dates it to the 1920s or 1930s, and a comparable machine at the Greer Heritage Museum (acc. 2002.1.1&2) is dated to the 1920s.[@ttw-susan-burr; @ttw-greer-susan-burr] Beside it in the Tuft the World collection is the All Steel Shuttle Needle of the Molter-Reinhard Co. of Chicago, made wholly of steel. One handle carries the needle point and the other a "blue steel looper", and the printed directions describe the same alternation: "insert needle point as far as it will go, then insert looper (which is the other handle)". The firm sold yarn and patterns too, as Ross had done ("You will be pleased with our rug patterns"). The package is undated.[@ttw-shuttle-needle]

Other inventors patented hand tools of their own. John E. Garrett of New Glasgow, Nova Scotia, patented a "Rug Hooker" in 1926 and a second in 1933; the first is very likely the Bluenose Rug Hooker,[@pat-us1605711a; @pat-us1941346a] which a Bucilla catalogue photographed at the Winterthur Library sold as "the fastest rug making innovation procurable today".[@wint-photo-bucilla-tools] E. J. Gearhart filed for a "Semiautomatic Turfing Device" in 1931, a spring-loaded punch needle by a later patent's account,[@pat-us1912977a; @pat-us3968757a] and Foster K. Solliday of Grand Rapids, Michigan, filed for a "Hooked Rug Making Device" in 1935.[@pat-us2077719a] By then a hand tool in Dalton had been given a motor.

## A motor in the handle, Dalton, 1932

The tufting machine had started from the sewing machine rather than the hand tool (chapter 5): in 1922 August J. Carter of Chattanooga filed for a "Tufting-Attachment Mechanism for Sewing Machines".[@ttw-carter-1922] Ten years later Lawrence P. Smith of Dalton filed, on 23 June 1932, for a "Motor Operated Tufting Machine", assigned to Cabin Crafts and granted on 4 July 1933. Its drawing shows a handheld tool with the motor in its body and the needle below, the earliest motorised hand tufting tool found in this research.[@pat-us1917162a]

<Figure id="pat-us1917162a" size="margin" caption="Lawrence P. Smith, “Motor Operated Tufting Machine,” US patent 1,917,162, filed 1932 and assigned to Cabin Crafts." />

Air came after the war, in two patents filed in June and July 1946. Samuel P. Parker's "Tufting Device", assigned to Callaway Mills of LaGrange, Georgia, was an air-fed handheld tool.[@pat-us2422126a] James H. Shay's "Hooked Rug Machine", assigned to Shaycraft Inc. of Newtown Square, Pennsylvania, drove a hand rug tool with compressed-air pistons, and it cites Ross's patent of 1891.[@pat-us2442906a]

Cut pile and a trigger followed. Melvin S. Hurley of Bridgend, England, filed in the United States in 1949 for a "Hand Tool for Making Rugs, Carpets and the Like with a Cut Pile", and a British patent of his for the same tool, so far seen only through search summaries, was published in 1951.[@pat-us2605727a; @pat-gb663196a] In October 1952 Hans G. Lustig of Lake Placid, New York, filed for a "Carpet Forming Device", a handheld tool with a trigger and a removable knife.[@pat-us2753820a] The tool did not yet have its name.

## Alfred Schauer's "hand gun-type device", 1962

<Figure id="pat-us3142276a" caption="Alfred Schauer, “Device for Forming Cut Pile Fabrics and the Like,” US patent 3,142,276, filed 1962: the patent that uses the phrase “tufting gun”." />

The word "gun" appears in the patent of Alfred Schauer of Elmhurst, New York, filed on 25 June 1962 and granted in 1964. Schauer called his tool a "hand gun-type device", used the phrase "tufting gun", and said that such hand guns "have been used for some time". It is the earliest use of the term found so far, and Schauer's remark places the guns themselves earlier still.[@pat-us3142276a; @ttw-tufting-gun-term] Helmut C. Mueller's patent for Bigelow-Sanford, filed in 1965, speaks of a "handheld tufting gun".[@pat-us3389667a; @ttw-tufting-gun-term]

Through the 1950s such tools had been patented as tufting machines or implements: by Theron F. Gifford of Chattanooga for the Ten-Tex Corp. (filed 1953), by Russell L. Sterner of Downey, California (1955), and by M. L. Ward and Lewis Teal for Callaway Mills (1956).[@pat-us2837045a; @pat-us2887076a; @pat-us2879731a] Cabin Crafts came back in 1962 with Charles D. Elliott's "Portable Machine for Tufting Cut Pile", "Hand carried and hand guided",[@pat-us3144844a] and Bigelow-Sanford was assigned O. J. Roberts's "Portable Tufting Machine", filed in 1964.[@pat-us3229653a]

Who used these guns, and for what, is less clear. The custom studios (chapter 11) made claims of their own: trade sources say Stanislav V'Soske, whose workshop was in Grand Rapids, Michigan, developed "the modern hand-tufting technique" in 1924,[@co-vsoske] and Edward Fields of New York says its founder developed a hand-tufting tool, the "Magic Needle", in the mid-1930s.[@co-edward-fields] Neither claim has yet been matched to a patent or a dated tool.

One use is named in a patent. In 1990 William H. Satterfield filed for a "Hand-held Tufting Mending Gun", a Dalton patent assigned to Spencer Wright Industries. Its list of prior art cites the hand guns of Lustig, Gifford, Ward, Sterner, Schauer, Elliott and Roberts.[@pat-us5090341a] Shaw Industries' design team has told Tim Eads, in conversation, that hand-held guns are still used on the broadloom line to fix mistakes found at final inspection; a written source for this is still to be found.[@finding-shaw-mending-gun] The two hand guns that a later patent would name as "traditional" were German.

## Hartmut Scholz's two patents, Allersberg

In Allersberg, in Bavaria, Hartmut Scholz filed in Germany for a "Device for tufting carpets by hand" on 13 November 1973.[@pat-de2356585c2] Its American counterpart, granted on 13 July 1976, describes a pneumatic hand gun: a hollow needle pierces the backing, the yarn is carried through it in an air stream, and one synchronous drive works both the cutter and the needle lift. It cites the American hand-gun patents of Schauer, Elliott and Mueller.[@pat-us3968758a]

  <Figure id="pat-us3968758a" caption="Hartmut Scholz, “Arrangement for the Tufting of Carpets,” US patent 3,968,758, 1976: a pneumatic hand gun with an air-fed hollow needle." />
  <Figure id="pat-us4029031a" caption="Hartmut Scholz, “Device for Providing Rugs and the Like with Tufts,” US patent 4,029,031, 1977: a manual two-arm tool, fed from a yarn cone." />
</Pair>

A company grew up around the gun. According to the history page of Hofmann Handtuft-Technik GmbH, now in Nördlingen, trials with the hand-tuft technique began in 1969, and Scholz Handtuft-Technik KG was founded in Allersberg near Nuremberg in 1973, with Hartmut Scholz and Heinz Hofmann as managing directors. Scholz left in 1979, and the firm took Hofmann's name. Company catalogue material dates the first machines to 1973 and their market launch to 1975.[@co-hofmann-handtuft]

Scholz's second patent family, with a German priority of 14 March 1975, went back to the hand tool. Its American member, granted in 1977, is a manual device with two arms worked alternately against the backing. One arm carries a fixed needle that guides the yarn; the other carries a blade that slides along the needle and cuts the yarn after each loop, closed by a spring. It cites Hurley's cut-pile hand tool.[@pat-us4029031a] This is Ross's two-part, alternate-stroke action with a blade added.

A second German gun is linked to Hans Hartleb, whose "hand tufting device" patent of 1978 has so far been seen only through search summaries.[@pat-de2815801a1] An Australian firm's patent, filed in 2008, describes "a traditional hand tufting gun for instance, Hofmann™ or Hartleb™, each of which are limited to tufting along an outline at a constant pile height". Its own subject, a computer-guided tufting head, belongs to chapter 12.[@pat-ep2077348b1] By a Hong Kong account, a gun was given a motor there in the year Scholz first filed.

## Tai Ping's gun, Hong Kong, 1973

Tai Ping Carpets of Hong Kong, founded in 1956, had moved from hand-knotting to hand-tufting, by its own account.[@co-tai-ping] Its general manager, the engineer Yip Yuen-cheung (Anthony Yeh), developed a new cut-pile tufting needle, drawing, says the Hong Kong Heritage Project's Chinese text, on the American method of making carpet with a single-needle sewing machine and on household tufting tools (家用簇絨工具).[@ner-hk-taiping-needle] The same source says that in 1973 Tai Ping fitted its tufting gun with a motor and made it lighter.[@ner-hk-taiping-1973]

The dates conflict. A 2021 trade article credits Yeh with a pneumatic gun in 1960, and Hong Kong heritage articles call the 1973 gun "the world's first" (全球首枝), a claim the American patents above contradict.[@ner-hk-taiping-1973; @ttw-conflict-gun-origin] In mainland China (chapter 8) a utility model for "A hand-held horizontal electric weaving needle for weaving carpets" was filed in April 2004, and a second, filed that November, describes an electric gun with a small motor, a gear-transmission box and a connecting rod.[@pat-cn2723478y; @pat-cn2806530y]

The unpowered tool never disappeared, and neither did the home tool. On 13 July 1976 the patent for Scholz's pneumatic gun was granted as US 3,968,758; the number before it went the same day to John S. Doyel's "Rug-tufting device suitable for home use by hobyists", a hand-operated tool "made primarily of molded plastic components".[@pat-us3968758a; @pat-us3968757a]

## Ronco's Speed-Tufting Kit, 1975

<Figure id="ttw-ronco-speed-tufting-kit" image={1} caption="Contents of the Ronco kit: the moulded-plastic tufting tool, fabric shredder, yarn, rug needle, latex sample, printed burlap backing and pattern sheet." />

In 1975 Ronco sold a "Speed-Tufting Kit" on television for $9.95. The box end says it was distributed exclusively by Ronco Teleproducts of Chicago, and the burlap backing inside is printed with an owl and "© 1975 Southbury Mfg. Corp.", the Connecticut maker.[@ttw-ronco-speed-tufting-kit] The tool is moulded plastic, with a needle handle and a looper handle worked in turn. The booklet explains: "With the Looper all the way up punch the needle through the backing material to form the first loop. Then push the Looper down to hold down the formed loop."[@ttw-ronco-speed-tufting-kit] The kit, like the Susan Burr, is in the collection of the author's firm.[@ttw-ronco-speed-tufting-kit]

The kit was built for home use from end to end. A fabric shredder cut old clothes, sheets or blankets into strips to tuft with, and an order sheet sold frames, latex and patterns by mail.[@ttw-ronco-speed-tufting-kit] Doyel's patent is very likely the one behind it.[@pat-us3968757a; @ttw-ronco-speed-tufting-kit] It was the Susan Burr's two-part action, needle and then holder in turn, about half a century on, in plastic.

Tools with the same alternating action are still sold new in Mirzapur, where, according to the author's research notes, Naksh Enterprises offers one for cut and loop pile up to 1.8 in (4.5 cm), at around ₹1,500, though commercial pile work there uses 220-volt electric guns (chapter 10).[@ttw-mirzapur-manual] The powered gun, meanwhile, became a product in its own right.

## The ZQ-III, from Shantou

The ZQ-III is a pneumatic tufting gun from Dayang, a carpet-machinery maker in Shantou, Guangdong. Dayang's website, seen so far only in search excerpts, calls it "a new portable type tufting machine" that combines an "electric & pneumatic device", and lists an electric tufting gun among its large tufting machines.[@tool-dayang-guns]

Other makers sell guns like it. Hofmann, whose beginnings are told above, still lists a pneumatic hand-tufting gun for cut and loop pile, the VML 16, according to its trade-fair listing as seen in search results.[@tool-hofmann-vml16] In Meerut, Sheru Bros Engineering Works, established in 1965 according to its own listing (another directory says fourteen years), advertises electric hand tufting guns on IndiaMART, one at about ₹9,000, and says it exports them to Egypt and South Africa. Every one of those details is the seller's own.[@ner-in-sheru] Studios use them too: Kramis in Switzerland makes bespoke rugs with single-needle tufting guns (chapter 11).[@co-kramis]

<Figure id="tool-ttw-electric-guns" size="margin" caption="Electric hand tufting guns sold by Tuft the World, the author’s firm." />

The guns also went on sale to individual makers. Tuft the World, the author's firm, created a store record for the ZQ-III in May 2018, though that is not a confirmed first-sale date. Its listing says the gun is "used in factory settings" and does both cut and loop pile.[@tool-ttw-pneumatic-guns] By the author's own account, its ZQ guns were made by Dayang, and its other guns are supplied under Tuft the World's own name.[@finding-ttw-gun-suppliers] Those electric guns, most of them about 3 lb (1.4 kg), come in models for cut pile, for loop pile and for both, and several of the store pages address beginners as well as advanced tufters.[@tool-ttw-electric-guns] Who bought them, and what came of it, is the subject of chapter 13.

This chapter argues that the tufting gun has no single inventor. It grew out of hand tools like Ross's, a needle with a second part to hold or cut the loop. In the patents found so far, American hand tools gained a motor in 1932, air in 1946, a trigger in 1952 and the name "tufting gun" by 1962. In the 1970s Scholz in Germany patented an air-fed gun on the principle of today's professional guns, and Tai Ping in Hong Kong, by the Hong Kong Heritage Project's account, fitted its gun with a motor, while the two-handled tool went on being made for the home. Today the powered gun is a product sold to factories, studios and beginners alike.[@tool-dayang-guns; @tool-ttw-pneumatic-guns; @co-kramis; @tool-ttw-electric-guns; @pat-us463548a; @pat-us1917162a; @pat-us2442906a; @pat-us2753820a; @pat-us3142276a; @pat-us3968758a; @ner-hk-taiping-1973; @pat-us3968757a]
