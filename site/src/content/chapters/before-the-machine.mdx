A bedcover at Winterthur carries a name and a year: "Mary Foot AD 1778".[@wint-1960-0594] It is wool, 80 by 80½ inches with a fringe, and it is recorded as needleworked pile with embroidery, probably made in Connecticut. It came to the museum in the bequest of Henry Francis du Pont.[@wint-1960-0594] It is the earliest dated piece of needle-made pile among the objects gathered for this book, and the place where the history of tufting in America can start.

## Three rugs for one family

Mary Foot's rug has relatives. Laurel Thatcher Ulrich, in *The Age of Homespun* (2001), links it to a bed rug marked "E.F.", for Elizabeth Foote, at the Connecticut Historical Society, and to an unmarked rug at Historic Deerfield.[@book-ulrich-age-of-homespun; @wint-1960-0594] All three may commemorate the marriages of the Foot siblings on 5 November 1778.[@wint-1960-0594] The "E.F." rug is wool and probably of the same year;[@chs-foote-bed-rug] the Deerfield rug is wool, blue and white, and dated about 1778.[@hd-blue-white-bed-rug]

The Deerfield rug shares its Tree of Life motif, described as Indian, with a bed rug of 1796 at the Metropolitan Museum of Art.[@hd-blue-white-bed-rug; @met-13-207] How the motif reached New England is still to be traced.

## A needle, not a hook

How these rugs were made matters more than what they were called. The Metropolitan Museum's bed rug of 1809, attributed to "M. B." and probably made in Connecticut, is wool, about 102 by 99 inches, and worked in a looped running stitch that was then cut to form pile.[@met-13-207] The museum's notes stress that the pile is needleworked, not hooked, a distinction once misunderstood.[@met-13-207] The Met acquired it in 1913.

That is the technique of the tufting machine in its simplest form. Tufting adds pile yarn to a backing that already exists: a needle pierces the backing and leaves loops, which can be cut.[@process-tufting-overview] A bed rug worker did the same by hand, drawing the yarn through a finished cloth and leaving a loop on the face at each stitch.[@met-13-207; @process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs]

<Figure id="wint-1964-1641" size="margin" caption="Bed rug marked “C/1819”, wool and linen, possibly from the Connecticut River Valley." />

Other dated rugs extend the run into the nineteenth century. Historic Deerfield holds a wool bed rug marked "Esther Packard 1801", attributed to Esther (Porter) Packard (1733–1812) and possibly made at Cummington, Massachusetts; its curator of textiles, David E. Lazaro, researched the rug when the museum acquired it, and the museum described that research in a blog post in 2017.[@hd-packard-1801] Winterthur has a second bed rug, marked "C/1819", of wool and linen, possibly from the Connecticut River Valley.[@wint-1964-1641] The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, holds another, catalogued as a "Bed rugg", whose date is still to be confirmed.[@mfa-1980-447]

The same stitch went onto the floor. A hearth rug at the Connecticut Historical Society, dated 1800–1820 and linked by family tradition to the Samuel Hanmer house in Wethersfield, is described as cut and uncut loops of wool sewn through plain-woven linen, with a boy and girl under a grapevine in an oval medallion and roses in the corners.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs] In the Getty vocabulary's definition of "yarn-sewn" rugs, as summarised for this record, yarn is sewn with a needle in a continuous running stitch through a closely woven cloth, leaving loops on the face that are usually clipped. That account comes from search results and is still to be checked against the museum's page; Colonial Williamsburg's laboratory reportedly found that most grounds in its exhibition of early sewn rugs were cotton, not linen, so each fibre needs checking.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs]

<Figure id="process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs" caption="Yarn-sewn hearth rug, 1800–1820, with a boy and girl under a grapevine in an oval medallion. Connecticut Historical Society." />

Bed rugs were brought together in 1972 for the Wadsworth Atheneum's loan exhibition in Hartford, open from 9 November to 31 December. Its 80-page catalogue, *Bed Ruggs/1722–1833*, dates the form by its title from 1722.[@book-bed-ruggs-1972]

## From wool to cotton: candlewick spreads

In the early nineteenth century the needle worked in cotton as well as wool. An Eagle Candlewick Spread of 1810–1820 at the American Folk Art Museum is cotton, worked with tufting and French knots; the museum notes that candlewicking combined flat stitches with raised ones, tufting among them, and that candlewick spreads recall the earlier yarn-sewn bed rugs.[@afam-1995-13-2]

Several are dated or named. The Los Angeles County Museum of Art has a cotton candlewick bedspread of 1821, about 96 by 83 inches, attributed to Mary Smith.[@lacma-m-74-48] The Connecticut Historical Society holds three cotton candlewick bed coverings with makers' names and dates: Thankful Stanton's of 1820, Esther M. C. Peck's of 1824 and Susannah Courtney's of 1825.[@chs-candlewick-group] Historic New England has a cotton spread with a vase and vine motif, dated 1820–1830, from Hamilton House at South Berwick, Maine.[@hne-1949-1018]

The word candlewick returns at the start of the Dalton story. Catherine Evans's spread of 1895 is described as a candlewick bedspread, made by sewing thick yarn into muslin and shrinking the cloth to lock the tufts (chapter 4).[@event-whitener-bedspread] What, if anything, joins the New England spreads of the 1820s to north Georgia in the 1890s is not yet documented.

## On the floor, and in the record

Needlework also made carpets. The "Caswell carpet" at the Metropolitan Museum was made by Zeruah H. Guernsey Caswell at Castleton, Vermont, in 1832–35, and is signed with her initials and dated 1835. It is wool, embroidered in chain stitch, and has a detachable hearth rug section.[@met-caswell-carpet] It is embroidery rather than pile. The Met questions how much of the base cloth and yarn Caswell made herself.[@met-caswell-carpet]

<Figure id="met-caswell-carpet" size="wide" caption="The “Caswell carpet”, wool embroidered in chain stitch, made by Zeruah H. Guernsey Caswell at Castleton, Vermont, 1832–35." />

The Caswell carpet was lent in 2007 to the American Folk Art Museum's exhibition "The Great Cover-Up: American Rugs on Beds, Tables, and Floors", which brought together yarn-sewn, shirred, appliquéd and embroidered early American rugs.[@afam-great-cover-up; @met-caswell-carpet] Its title is a reminder that in early America a rug could lie on a bed or a table as well as on a floor.

The word "tufted" itself appears on a later record. About 1938 Orville A. Carroll painted a watercolour of a "Shaker Tufted Wool Rug" in Kentucky for the Index of American Design.[@wc-82212663] What "tufted" means on that rug, whether sewn tufts, shirring or yarn-sewn pile, is not stated, and the rug's own date is not known.[@wc-82212663]

<Figure id="wc-82212663" size="margin" caption="Orville A. Carroll, “Shaker Tufted Wool Rug”, watercolour for the Index of American Design, Kentucky, about 1938." />

This chapter argues that pile sewn with a needle through a finished cloth, the principle of tufting, was practised in New England from at least 1778, on bed rugs marked with women's names, and was carried into cotton on the candlewick spreads of the 1810s and 1820s. The machine did not invent the technique.
