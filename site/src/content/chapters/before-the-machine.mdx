A bedcover at the Winterthur Museum, 80 by 80.5 inches, is inscribed with a name and a year: "Mary Foot AD 1778". It is a needle-made pile textile, probably made in Connecticut, and it came to the museum in the bequest of Henry Francis du Pont.[@wint-1960-0594] This chapter starts from it and asks how much of what a tufting machine does was already being done by hand, on beds, more than a century before Dalton.

## The Foot family's rugs, 1778

Mary Foot's rug may be one of three. Laurel Thatcher Ulrich, in *The Age of Homespun* (2001), links it to a bed rug marked "E.F.", for Elizabeth Foote, at the Connecticut Historical Society, and to an unmarked rug at Historic Deerfield.[@book-ulrich-age-of-homespun; @wint-1960-0594] All three may commemorate the marriages of the Foot siblings on 5 November 1778.[@wint-1960-0594] The "E.F." rug is probably of the same year, and the Deerfield rug, blue and white, is dated about 1778.[@chs-foote-bed-rug; @hd-blue-white-bed-rug]

The Deerfield rug looks outward in its design. It shares its Tree of Life motif, described as Indian, with a bed rug of 1796 at the Metropolitan Museum of Art.[@hd-blue-white-bed-rug; @met-13-207] How the motif reached New England is still to be traced.

The Foot rugs sit within a longer run. When bed rugs were brought together for a loan exhibition in Hartford, open from 9 November to 31 December 1972, the Wadsworth Atheneum's 80-page catalogue was titled *Bed Ruggs/1722–1833*; by that title, the survey reaches back to 1722.[@book-bed-ruggs-1972] These records say little about how the pile was made. A later rug at the Metropolitan Museum is more explicit.

## The Metropolitan Museum's bed rug of 1809

This rug, about 102 by 99 inches, is attributed to "M. B." and was probably made in Connecticut. It came to the museum through the Rogers Fund in 1913.[@met-13-207] The museum's notes stress one point about it: the pile is needleworked, not hooked, a distinction once misunderstood.[@met-13-207]

That point is the technique of the tufting machine in its simplest form. In the Britannica student encyclopedia's account, as summarised for this book from search results still to be checked against the page, tufting adds pile yarn to a backing that already exists: a needle pierces the backing and leaves loops, which can be cut.[@process-tufting-overview] A bed rug worker did the same by hand. In the Getty vocabulary's definition of "yarn-sewn" work, as summarised from search results and still to be checked, yarn is sewn with a needle in a continuous running stitch through a closely woven cloth, leaving loops on the face that are usually clipped; the same record says the Met's bed rugs use that needle-worked running stitch.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs; @met-13-207]

The stitch went onto the floor as well, in the yarn-sewn hearth rug that family tradition links to Wethersfield, described in chapter 1.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs] On the bed, other rugs of the period carry names or initials and dates.

## Two marked rugs: "Esther Packard 1801" and "C/1819"

<Figure id="wint-1964-1641" size="margin" caption="Bed rug marked “C/1819”, possibly from the Connecticut River Valley. Winterthur Museum." />

Historic Deerfield holds a bed rug marked "Esther Packard 1801". It is attributed to Esther (Porter) Packard (1733–1812) and was possibly made at Cummington, Massachusetts.[@hd-packard-1801] When the museum acquired it, its curator of textiles, David E. Lazaro, researched the rug, and the museum described that research in a blog post in 2017.[@hd-packard-1801]

Winterthur has a second bed rug, marked "C/1819", possibly from the Connecticut River Valley.[@wint-1964-1641] The record names no maker. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, holds another, catalogued as a "Bed rugg", whose date is still to be confirmed.[@mfa-1980-447]

The marks give the bed rugs their chronology, and they run on into the 1810s. By then the needle was also working raised stitches into a different kind of bedcover.

## Candlewick spreads in Connecticut, 1820 to 1825

<Figure id="chs-candlewick-group" caption="Candlewick bed covering at the Connecticut Historical Society, from its group of maker-named pieces dated 1820 to 1825." />

The Connecticut Historical Society holds three candlewick bed coverings with makers' names and dates: Thankful Stanton's of 1820, Esther M. C. Peck's of 1824 and Susannah Courtney's of 1825.[@chs-candlewick-group] They are cotton, a dated sequence of early American tufted whitework.[@chs-candlewick-group]

The American Folk Art Museum's Eagle Candlewick Spread, of 1810–1820, shows what the stitch was doing. The museum notes that candlewicking combined flat stitches with dimensional ones, tufting among them, and that candlewick spreads recall the earlier yarn-sewn bed rugs.[@afam-1995-13-2] Others are dated to the 1820s. The Los Angeles County Museum of Art has a candlewick bedspread of 1821 attributed to Mary Smith,[@lacma-m-74-48] and Historic New England has one with a vase and vine motif, dated 1820–1830, from Hamilton House at South Berwick, Maine.[@hne-1949-1018]

The word returns at the start of the Dalton story. Catherine Evans's spread of about 1895–1900 is described as a candlewick bedspread, made by sewing thick yarn into muslin and shrinking the cloth to lock the tufts (chapter 4).[@event-whitener-bedspread] What, if anything, joins the New England spreads of the 1820s to north Georgia in the 1890s is not yet documented. In the meantime, needlework had also reached carpet size.

## Zeruah Caswell's carpet, Castleton, Vermont

<Figure id="met-caswell-carpet" size="wide" caption="The “Caswell carpet”, an embroidered carpet made by Zeruah H. Guernsey Caswell at Castleton, Vermont, 1832–35. Metropolitan Museum of Art." />

The "Caswell carpet" at the Metropolitan Museum was made by Zeruah H. Guernsey Caswell at Castleton, Vermont, in 1832–35, and she signed it with her initials and dated it 1835. It is catalogued as an embroidered carpet, and it has a detachable hearth rug section.[@met-caswell-carpet] The Met questions how much of the base cloth and yarn Caswell made herself.[@met-caswell-carpet]

In 2007 the carpet was lent to the American Folk Art Museum's exhibition "The Great Cover-Up: American Rugs on Beds, Tables, and Floors", which brought together yarn-sewn, shirred, appliquéd and embroidered early American rugs.[@afam-great-cover-up; @met-caswell-carpet] The exhibition's title makes the point this chapter depends on: in early America a rug could lie on a bed or a table as well as on a floor. About a century later, an artist recorded a rug under the word "tufted".

## A watercolour of a Shaker rug, about 1938

<Figure id="wc-82212663" size="margin" caption="Orville A. Carroll, “Shaker Tufted Wool Rug”, watercolour for the Index of American Design, Kentucky, about 1938." />

About 1938 Orville A. Carroll painted a watercolour of a "Shaker Tufted Wool Rug" for the Index of American Design in Kentucky; the National Gallery of Art has since given the image to Wikimedia Commons.[@wc-82212663] The record dates the painting, not the rug. Nor does it say what "tufted" means on it, whether sewn tufts, shirring or yarn-sewn pile.[@wc-82212663] It leaves open the question of what joined the hand-sewn pile of New England to the tufted bedspreads of north Georgia.

This chapter argues that the principle of tufting, pile sewn with a needle through a finished cloth, was practised in New England from at least 1778, on bed rugs marked with women's names, and was carried onto the candlewick spreads of the 1810s and 1820s. The machine did not invent the technique: women had worked it by hand, on the bed, long before.
