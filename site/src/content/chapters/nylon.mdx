Tufting supplied the machine; nylon supplied the material. The chenille bedspread machines of north Georgia were widened to make rugs and then broadloom carpet.[@chenille-widened] This chapter follows the fibre that went into them, and the long campaign that taught Americans to want it underfoot.

## A fibre from the laboratory

The word needs care. The fibre now called nylon 6,6 came out of Wallace Carothers's research at DuPont, which the company patented in 1937 ("Linear condensation polymers", granted 16 February) and 1938 ("Synthetic fiber").[@pat-us2071250a; @pat-us2130948a] Hagley, which holds DuPont's archives, marks the first of those dates as the day the company was granted its nylon patent.[@hagley-vault-1937-patent] The standard account of how DuPont turned Carothers's polymer into a product is Hounshell and Smith's *Science and Corporate Strategy*.[@book-hounshell-smith]

A second nylon, nylon 6, was developed separately by Paul Schlack at IG Farben in Germany in 1938, and it too went into carpet: Allied Chemical's Caprolan, a nylon 6, competed with DuPont's fibre in tufted broadloom.[@ttw-nylon-6-schlack] "DuPont invented nylon" is true, then, only of nylon 6,6.[@ttw-claim-dupont-nylon]

DuPont's first nylon plant, at Seaford, Delaware, dates from 1939. Carpet came later: Hagley's guide to the Seaford collection says nylon for the carpet industry was developed there in 1958.[@hagley-seaford-nylon]

## First on the floor, and woven

Nylon reached the floor woven before it was tufted. DuPont used its own hotel in Wilmington as a showcase. A press release of 13 July 1948 reports that "a cut pile rug of burgundy spun nylon in Axminster weave was laid nine months ago in the Brandywine Room", one of the Hotel du Pont's restaurants, and that a Wilton carpet in a gray and white spiral pattern was planned for the Grill Room.[@u-mvs3g9dd3zmb0]

<Figure id="u-mvs3jcd5g72ua" size="wide" caption="The Nylon Suite at the Hotel DuPont, Wilmington. On the floor, James Lees and Sons’ “Sculptex” carpet with an all-nylon face. Hagley dates the photograph about 1945; DuPont’s 1948 press release suggests a later date." />

Photographs of the hotel's "Nylon Suite" show James Lees and Sons' "Sculptex" carpet: "an all nylon face in grey-green", which, the caption adds, "does not attract moths or other insects".[@u-mvs3jcd5g72ua]

In May 1948 DuPont announced a first. The carpet came from the Nye-Wait Company of Auburn, New York, under the name "Splendante", its pile woven in contrasting heights to give "a bas-relief effect", in beige, gray and pastel green.[@u-mvs379gngi80e]

<PullQuote from="u-mvs379gngi80e" who="DuPont press release, 12 May 1948">The first carpet on the market with pile completely woven of Du Pont nylon</PullQuote>

About 1955 Nye-Wait showed almost a hundred nylon carpet samples to the press in New York, all available nationally. DuPont's caption called nylon carpeting "paradoxically the most luxurious yet in terms of wear life and upkeep, the most economical ever made", and dated its introduction to five years before.[@u-mvs37wl6gkh9w]

<Figure id="hagley-nylon-hooked-rug" size="margin" caption="Nancy du Pont Reynolds Cooch’s nylon hooked rug, 1950." />

Nylon was tried by hand as well as by loom. In 1950 Nancy du Pont Reynolds Cooch made a hooked rug in nylon, 116 by 139 inches, which Hagley links to DuPont's wear-testing of nylon for floor coverings; walked on, it frayed and pilled.[@hagley-nylon-hooked-rug] It is a rare handmade bridge between rug-making at home and synthetic carpet, though not the only nylon hooked rug: Hagley also holds one in nylon and cotton from 1975–1990.[@hagley-2015-10-2]

Keeping carpet clean was a project of its own. About 1955 DuPont photographed a test strip of carpet partly treated with Ludox colloidal silica: after vacuuming, "treated areas emerge clean, while untreated portions remain dirty and dingy". A companion photograph said Ludox "is rapidly being adopted by the carpet industry as a soil retardant".[@hagley-1972341-ludox]

<Figure id="u-mvs37zoguerdx" caption="A bath mat and lid cover “tufted with DuPont nylon,” about 1955." />

And nylon was already being tufted. A DuPont photograph of about the same date shows a bath mat and matching lid cover "tufted with DuPont nylon", which their maker described as machine washable.[@u-mvs37zoguerdx]

## Made for tufting: "501" and BCF

The fibre that made tufted broadloom took its working form in the late 1950s. Tom McAndrews, who worked in DuPont's textile fibres business, recalled in a 2018 oral history that DuPont made carpet filament from 1952 and launched "501" carpet nylon, with a special crimp, in 1958.[@hagley-oh-mcandrews]

<Figure id="u-mvs3387y549fp" size="margin" caption="Nylon: The First 25 Years, DuPont’s anniversary booklet, 1963." />

The form that mattered most was bulked continuous filament, or BCF. Later histories date it variously to 1957 and 1959. DuPont's own chronology, in its anniversary booklet *Nylon: The First 25 Years* (1963), gives 1958: "A special bulked continuous filament nylon yarn was developed for carpeting; it soon revolutionized the market for rugs and carpeting."[@event-bcf-nylon; @u-mvs3387y549fp]

The market moved quickly. Tufted carpet sales, near zero in the late 1940s, passed 100 million square yards by 1958.[@article-patton-ehnet] Tufted carpet reportedly sold wholesale at about half the price of woven.[@tufted-half-price] By 1963 DuPont's booklet could claim that "nylon for carpets now totals over 150 million pounds a year", and that the continuous type had helped nylon take "over 26 per cent of market for broadloom carpeting".[@u-mvs3387y549fp, image 25]

The old weavers moved too. Mohawk and Alexander Smith, two of the great woven-carpet firms, merged as Mohasco in 1956.[@mohasco-1956] Its 1957 annual report describes new synthetic fibres in its lines, Chromspun acetate and Acrilan acrylic, and new manufacturing techniques for its tufted and velvet fabrics, for which "patents have been received or are pending".[@u-mvs3lacgfbrui, page image 7]

## Selling a synthetic floor

A new fibre had to be sold twice: to the trade, and to the people who would walk on it. In 1952 Ernest Dichter's Institute for Motivational Research had proposed a psychological study of "the Sales and Advertising Problems" of the Carpet Institute, the wool carpet industry's trade body.[@u-mvs3rwdznyj3f] For DuPont, Dichter studied why shoppers resisted or embraced nylon carpet,[@hagley-dichter-nylon-carpets] and in November 1957 he proposed a second study, of its acceptance among distributors, buyers and retailers.[@u-mvs3aug7hh3lv] In January 1959 DuPont's own market researchers reported on "Retailers' Attitudes Toward Carpet Fibers and the Du Pont Airwaves Program Promoting 100% Nylon Carpeting".[@u-mvs3riubri1f6]

The airwaves were literal. DuPont sponsored network drama, and "DuPont Carpet Nylon" commercials ran in its breaks: in *The DuPont Show with June Allyson* on 5 October 1959 and 11 January 1960;[@hagley-film-opening-door-1959; @u-mvs3903rv1gjh] in *The DuPont Show of the Month* in 1960, in "The Way Home" with Ronald Reagan;[@hagley-film-fc319] with June Allyson again on 20 February 1961;[@u-mvs393k4f607p] and, as "DuPont 501 Carpet Nylon", in *The DuPont Show of the Week* in 1962.[@u-mvs3970mvyiw9]

In print the campaign ran in the *New York Times Magazine* in 1960[@u-mvs38atad1qhj] and on through the mid-1960s, from "A Houseful of Value ... All '501'" (1965) to "Those Five Tough DuPont Tests" (1966).[@u-mvs38hqlyz4s6; @u-mvs38l6olbged] One advertisement of 1966 was headed:

<PullQuote from="u-mvs38omy32ao5" who="DuPont advertisement, 1966">Don’t Let Anyone Mislead You, DuPont 501 Is Not the Name of a Fiber</PullQuote>

Designers showed the carpet in rooms. At the 1962 Decoration & Design Show in New York, William Pahlmann Associates laid wall-to-wall carpet of "continuous filament nylon in white and beige" by James Lees & Sons, named "Happy Day".[@hagley-1972430-1080] Competitors answered with their own fibres and their own advertising, among them Allied Chemical's Caprolan.[@duke-adviews-caprolan]

## Antron, and after

In 1967 DuPont introduced a new carpet fibre under the headline "Meet Antron: The New Carpet Fiber for Enduring Elegance".[@hagley-dpads-1803-00680] By 1969 its advertisements called Antron "the Luxury Carpet Fiber from DuPont".[@hagley-dpads-1803-00709; @hagley-dpads-1803-00712] Sarah Archer has written about this advertising and the image of luxury it sold.[@article-archer-luxury-underfoot]

The same year a DuPont chemist, Donald Hull, invented conductive fibres to control static in nylon carpet.[@hagley-hull-papers] The promotion could be spectacular: for the 1977 New York City Marathon, DuPont laid Antron carpet on a bridge for the runners.[@hagley-film-fc249-02]

Nylon carried other names into American homes too. A rug labelled "Winterthur Collection", 100 per cent nylon and made by Shaw Rugs under licence from the Henry Francis du Pont Winterthur Museum, has appeared at auction.[@wint-shaw-licensed-nylon] The du Pont name had come full circle, from the laboratory to the period room.

The argument of this chapter is simple. Tufting supplied the machine and nylon the material. The broadloom boom was the two together, and a decade of advertising made it something every household wanted.
