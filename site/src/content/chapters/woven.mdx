On 30 May 1800 the London auctioneer James Christie offered 150 lots of carpet in one sale. His eight-page catalogue named four kinds: Brussels, Wilton, Venetian and Kidderminster, all of them woven on the loom. A microfilm of it is at the New York Public Library and has not yet been seen for this book.[@book-christie-1800-carpet-sale] This chapter follows those carpets from their beginnings to the industry that tufting displaced.

## Knotted carpet, the costly standard

The model was French and knotted by hand. Pierre Dupont is said to have set up a workshop in a former soap factory at Chaillot around 1615, and in 1627 Louis XIII granted him and his former apprentice Simon Lourdet an eighteen-year monopoly on carpets "in the Turkish manner".[@savonnerie-1627] The Metropolitan Museum's carpet of 1680, after designs by Charles Le Brun, is one of the series woven for the Grande Galerie of the Louvre: knotted wool pile, 896.6 by 325.1 centimetres. By Sotheby's count Dupont's workshop made 32 of the series and the Lourdet workshops 60.[@met-1976-155-114-savonnerie]

<Figure id="met-1976-155-114-savonnerie" size="wide" caption="Savonnerie carpet for the Grande Galerie of the Louvre, after designs by Charles Le Brun, 1680: knotted wool pile, 896.6 by 325.1 cm." />

In the 1750s Savonnerie weavers came to London. Peter Parisot employed workers from Chaillot, first at Paddington and then at Fulham, with money from the Duke of Cumberland. The Victoria County History records that his goods were judged too costly, and his stock was auctioned in January 1756; the venture's start is dated 1750 in one account and 1753 in another.[@fulham-parisot-1750] Claude Passavant, a Swiss Huguenot, moved the weavers to Exeter, where, according to Axminster Heritage, the works stopped trading in 1761 because its carpets were too expensive.[@exeter-passavant-1756]

Thomas Whitty, who had run a cloth business since 1737, finished his first knotted carpet at Axminster at midsummer 1755.[@axminster-whitty-1755] The Society of Arts then offered prizes for English "Turkey" carpets. In 1757 Thomas Moore of Moorfields made the finest carpet, but at forty guineas; Whitty's, at £15, was judged the best value, and the prize was split. Whitty won outright in 1759.[@rsa-carpet-premiums-1757-59] Moore also wove to Robert Adam's designs: the Soane Museum describes the drawing-room carpet at Osterley as "executed in accordance with Adam's design by Thomas Moore of Moorfields".[@moorfields-moore]

In 2025 the Met acquired a knotted wool carpet attributed to Whitty and dated about 1765. The museum gives his dates as 1713 to 1792, though other accounts give 1716 for his birth.[@met-928622; @axminster-whitty-1755] Its other Whitty carpet, "probably" his, dates from about 1780 to 1790.[@met-57-162-axminster]

## Flat carpet: Kidderminster, Scotch and Venetian

Below the knotted carpet sat a flat one. In 1735, the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* says, Kidderminster's woollen trade "was however replaced by that of carpets", and at first the town made only "Kidderminster" carpets. Local historians credit John Pearsall with the first, or else two stuff weavers. By 1772, according to the 1797 *Britannica*, the town had over 250 carpet looms.[@woven-kidderminster-1735] The cloth was double: a mid-nineteenth-century sample at the Victoria and Albert Museum has a worsted warp and a woollen weft, "a construction particular to this type of carpet", and the 1842 *Britannica* describes it as "composed of two plies of cloth", the colours reversed on the back.[@vam-t5-1959-kidderminster-sample]

<Figure id="vam-t5-1959-kidderminster-sample" size="margin" caption="Kidderminster carpeting in double cloth with a star-and-cross design, by Dove & Co. of Leeds, mid-19th century." />

The same cloth had other names. One local history says it was called ingrain, then Kidderminster, then Scotch once the process moved to Scotland.[@woven-kidderminster-1735] A single source dates carpet-making at Kilmarnock to 1728; it has not been confirmed elsewhere. A museum account describes Kilmarnock's early Scotch carpets as pileless cloth rugs, sold largely to Holland.[@woven-scotch-kilmarnock]

Vanessa Habib has used Thomas Gilfillan's cash book and ledger of 1764 to 1770 as rare evidence of a working Scotch carpet manufactory at Stirling. She quotes an observer of 1776 on whom they suited:[@article-habib-scotch-stirling]

<PullQuote from="article-habib-scotch-stirling" who="An observer of 1776, quoted by Vanessa Habib">cannot afford the extravagant prices of Wilton, Axminster, and other more expensive manufactories</PullQuote>

Venetian carpet was the other flat kind. The 1842 *Britannica* says it was used mostly for stairs, passages and lobbies, the plain sorts usually striped in colours shading from dark to light.[@woven-venetian-carpet]

## Pile on the loom: Brussels and Wilton

Brussels carpet raised its pile on the loom. In the 1842 *Britannica*'s account, long steel wires were inserted between the linen and worsted warps, looping up the worsted pile; Brussels and Wilton were woven "in looms of the same construction".[@woven-brussels-wire-loop] The National Park Service adds that Brussels was woven in strips on a 27-inch loom. Wilton was the same carpet with the loops cut, giving a velvet surface: its wires had a groove along which a knife, the "travat", was run.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop; @woven-wilton-cutting-wire]

When the loops were first cut at Wilton is disputed. The charter that Wilton's weavers sought in 1699 concerned the woollen-cloth trade, not carpet.[@woven-wilton-1699-charter] All accounts credit the 9th Earl of Pembroke with bringing French weavers to Wilton, smuggled in wine barrels in one legend; the town's heritage trail says 1741, the firm Wilton Carpets 1721.[@woven-wilton-french-weavers] The Victoria County History gives the first written evidence of carpet at Wilton as a patent of 1741 to Ignatius Couran, John Barford and William Moody for carpeting "known as Moccadoes". It notes that the patented loom had no device for cutting the loops, and points to French moquettes as their likelier origin. The firm credits a weaver it calls Defosse with the cutting.[@woven-wilton-1741-patent-moccadoes; @wilton-1741] The first known use of "Wilton" for the carpet is dated 1774.[@woven-wilton-cutting-wire]

Kidderminster set up a Brussels loom in 1749.[@woven-kidderminster-brussels-1749] Tradition says John Broom "travelled to Brussels, Tournai, or Wilton" and came back with the plans and the workers; the local-history site that tells it warns that none of the versions can be proved and all suggest industrial espionage. One account has Broom and Pearsall putting up a building with 32 looms at Mount Skippet that March. The two towns became rivals.[@woven-kidderminster-brussels-1749; @woven-wilton-1741-patent-moccadoes]

## American floors

America imported first. The National Park Service finds Brussels and Wilton in American inventories and advertisements from the 1750s.[@nps-wilton-america] But it also says that by the last quarter of the century most middle-class houses had inexpensive Scotch carpeting, floorcloths or straw mats, and quotes Tobias Lear, writing for George Washington in 1790: "Scotch Carpeting is almost the only kind to be found here in New York".[@woven-scotch-carpet-america-1790]

By the Park Service's account America's first full commercial carpet factory was William Peter Sprague's in Philadelphia (1791), and it knotted carpet in the Axminster manner. Ingrain came to American mills later: the Tariff Manufacturing Company at Simsbury, Connecticut, incorporated in 1825, and Orrin Thompson's works at Thompsonville, founded in 1828, which made "inexpensive flat-woven ingrain carpeting" and became Bigelow-Hartford.[@woven-us-ingrain-1820s] The Tariff company dyed for pile carpet too: two notebooks of recipes for dyeing wool for Brussels carpets at its mills, from about 1840, are at the Winterthur Library. The mills closed in 1867 after a fire.[@wint-lib-tariff-brussels] An ingrain fragment at Historic New England keeps both selvages, showing the narrow loom width that was sewn into room-size carpet.[@hne-1959-7b-ingrain]

## Pattern and power

On the draw loom, a second worker, the drawboy, selected the warp ends for each pattern pick. Britannica dates Joseph-Marie Jacquard's punched-card attachment to 1804–05 (another account says 1801). A Worcester local history says it reached Kidderminster by 1825, and another says it replaced the "mounture" of the Brussels loom and was hard for small loom shops to afford, hastening the move out of the cottages.[@drawloom-to-jacquard-carpet]

The draw-loom method survives on paper from just before that change. Michelle McFadden's 1993 Winterthur Program thesis studies 46 watercolour drafts for five-frame Brussels or Wilton carpet, signed and dated by I. Arbuthnot in 1803–06 and 1813–17, with weaving instructions on the front and colours and quantities on the back. Where the drafts are now is not yet known.[@thesis-mcfadden-arbuthnot-drafts]

Then came power. The Smithsonian holds Erastus Bigelow's patent model of a loom, dated 1838.[@si-bigelow-loom-model] Bigelow built power looms for ingrain about 1839–41 (sources vary between 1839, 1841 and 1843) and then for Brussels and Wilton, between 1845 and 1851; accounts tie them to cheaper production, with lower-paid women and boys in place of male weavers.[@bigelow-power-loom] The power Brussels loom went into production at the Bigelow mill in Clinton, Massachusetts, which dates from 1848.[@bigelow-mill] One popular source says Bigelow introduced the first broadloom carpet in 1877; it is uncorroborated.[@bigelow-broadloom-1877]

The knotted carpet's name returned on a machine. Halcyon Skinner is credited with perfecting the power loom for "Royal Axminster" in 1876, for Alexander Smith & Sons of Yonkers, a claim still to be corroborated.[@skinner-axminster-1876] The machine Axminster only borrowed the name.[@axminster-whitty-1755] A spool Axminster loom photographed at Shaw Industries in Dalton carries tube frames overhead, each wound with one row of coloured pile yarn; the loom inserts a row of tufts from one spool, cuts them and weaves them in. The identification rests on the photograph alone.[@own-spool-axminster-loom]

## The woven industry meets tufting

By one account, carpet sales fell in the mid-1920s and woven firms such as Bigelow and Mohawk struggled before the Depression.[@woven-slump-1920s] Mohawk's founders, the Shuttleworths, came from England in 1875 and wove carpet at Amsterdam, New York, on secondhand looms; a merger of 1920 made Mohawk Carpet Mills.[@co-mohawk] Its 1956 merger with Alexander Smith, which made Mohasco, is told in chapter 6.[@mohasco-1956] Mohasco's annual report for 1957 already describes "tufted and velvet fabric lines".[@u-mvs3lacgfbrui, page image 7] The company then moved south, to a tufted plant at Dublin, Georgia, and weaving of Wilton and velvet at Laurens, South Carolina, had ended by 1965; carpet making at Amsterdam ended about 1968.[@co-mohawk]

The rival was cheaper. Tufted carpet reportedly sold wholesale at about half the price of woven,[@tufted-half-price] and, according to the Carpet and Rug Institute, on machinery developed for chenille rugs and then widened to make broadloom (chapter 4).[@chenille-widened] Langhorne Carpet in Pennsylvania, noted in the research for this book as one of the last American Wilton mills, has since closed; the date and a published source are still to be traced.[@langhorne-wilton]

The woven industry's story continues in Bigelow-Hartford's *A Century of Carpet and Rug Making in America* (1925),[@book-bigelow-century] Ewing and Norton's *Broadlooms and Businessmen* (1955), written from Bigelow-Sanford's records,[@book-ewing-norton-broadlooms-1955] and William Reynolds's *Innovation in the United States Carpet Industry, 1947–1963*, on the years tufting overtook weaving.[@book-reynolds-innovation-1968]

This chapter argues that tufting entered a market the loom had already made. From the 1730s British and then American looms made carpet for the cheap end of the market as well as the dear, from flat Scotch cloth to Wilton pile, and it was this woven industry that tufted carpet, reportedly at about half its wholesale price, undercut.
