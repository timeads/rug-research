On 30 May 1800 the London auctioneer James Christie offered 150 lots of carpet in a single sale. His eight-page catalogue named four kinds, Brussels, Wilton, Venetian and Kidderminster, and every one of them was woven on the loom. A microfilm of the catalogue is at the New York Public Library; it has not yet been seen for this book.[@book-christie-1800-carpet-sale] This chapter traces how the loom came to supply a whole market, from the cheap floor to the dear one, and how that market was the one tufted carpet later took.

## A carpet for the Grande Galerie, 1680

<Figure id="met-1976-155-114-savonnerie" size="wide" caption="Savonnerie carpet for the Grande Galerie of the Louvre, after designs by Charles Le Brun, 1680: knotted wool pile, 896.6 by 325.1 cm. Metropolitan Museum of Art." />

The standard that the loom had to answer was French and knotted by hand. The Metropolitan Museum's carpet of 1680, after designs by Charles Le Brun, is one of the series woven for the Grande Galerie of the Louvre: knotted wool pile, nearly nine metres long. By Sotheby's count, Pierre Dupont's workshop made 32 of the series and the Lourdet workshops 60.[@met-1976-155-114-savonnerie] Dupont is said to have set up a workshop in a former soap factory at Chaillot around 1615, and in 1627 Louis XIII granted him and his former apprentice Simon Lourdet an eighteen-year monopoly on carpets "in the Turkish manner".[@savonnerie-1627]

In the 1750s that model was tried in England, and it proved too dear. Peter Parisot employed workers from Chaillot, first at Paddington and then at Fulham, with money from the Duke of Cumberland. The Victoria County History records that his goods were judged too costly, and his stock was auctioned in January 1756; the venture's start is dated 1750 in one account and 1753 in another.[@fulham-parisot-1750] Claude Passavant, a Swiss Huguenot, moved the weavers to Exeter, where, according to Axminster Heritage, the works stopped trading in 1761 because its carpets were too expensive.[@exeter-passavant-1756] At Axminster, meanwhile, another knotter was competing on price.

## Thomas Whitty's carpet, about 1765

The carpet in the picture at the head of this chapter is knotted wool, 17 feet by almost 15, attributed to Thomas Whitty of the Axminster carpet manufactory and dated about 1765. The Metropolitan Museum acquired it in 2025.[@met-928622] Whitty had run a cloth business since 1737, and he finished his first knotted carpet at Axminster at midsummer 1755.[@axminster-whitty-1755]

Price was what set him apart. The Society of Arts offered prizes for English "Turkey" carpets, and in 1757 Thomas Moore of Moorfields made the finest carpet, but at forty guineas. Whitty's, at £15, was judged the best value, and the prize was split; in 1759 Whitty won outright.[@rsa-carpet-premiums-1757-59] Moore also made carpets to Robert Adam's designs, among them the drawing-room carpet at Osterley.[@moorfields-moore] Hand-knotted carpet, even at Whitty's prices, sat at the top of the market. Below it, Britain's looms were already making a flat carpet.

## A sample of Kidderminster cloth

<Figure id="vam-t5-1959-kidderminster-sample" size="margin" caption="Kidderminster carpeting in double cloth with a star-and-cross design, by Dove & Co. of Leeds, mid-19th century. Victoria and Albert Museum." />

The Victoria and Albert Museum keeps a mid-nineteenth-century sample of Kidderminster carpeting by Dove & Co. of Leeds, with a star-and-cross design. It is woven in double cloth with a worsted warp and a woollen weft, "a construction particular to this type of carpet". The 1842 *Britannica* described such carpet as "composed of two plies of cloth", with the colours reversed on the back.[@vam-t5-1959-kidderminster-sample] It has no pile at all.

The town it is named for turned to this cloth in 1735. That year, the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* says, Kidderminster's woollen trade "was however replaced by that of carpets", and at first the town made only "Kidderminster" carpets. Local historians credit John Pearsall with the first, or else two stuff weavers. By 1772, according to the 1797 *Britannica*, the town had over 250 carpet looms.[@woven-kidderminster-1735] The same cloth went by other names: one local history says it was called ingrain, then Kidderminster, then Scotch once the process moved to Scotland.[@woven-kidderminster-1735]

Its market is described in a study of a Scottish account book. Vanessa Habib has used Thomas Gilfillan's cash book and ledger of 1764 to 1770 as rare evidence of a working Scotch carpet manufactory at Stirling, and she quotes an observer of 1776 on whom such carpets suited:[@article-habib-scotch-stirling]

<PullQuote from="article-habib-scotch-stirling" who="An observer of 1776, quoted by Vanessa Habib">cannot afford the extravagant prices of Wilton, Axminster, and other more expensive manufactories</PullQuote>

Venetian carpet, the fourth of Christie's kinds, was flat too; the 1842 *Britannica* says it was used mostly for stairs, passages and lobbies.[@woven-venetian-carpet] Above these flat cloths, the loom had also found a way to make pile.

## Two samples: Brussels and Wilton

<Pair>
  <Figure id="vam-t11-1981-garthwaite-brussels" caption="Brussels carpet sample woven in 1980 by Hugh Mackay & Co. after a design by Anna Maria Garthwaite dated 1753." />
  <Figure id="woven-wilton-cutting-wire" caption="“Lily” Wilton carpet sample, designed by William Morris in 1875 and woven by the Wilton Royal Carpet Factory." />
</Pair>

Two more samples at the Victoria and Albert Museum show the loom's pile. One is a Brussels carpet sample woven in 1980 by Hugh Mackay & Co. after a design by Anna Maria Garthwaite dated 1753.[@vam-t11-1981-garthwaite-brussels] The other is the "Lily" Wilton sample, designed by William Morris in 1875 and woven by the Wilton Royal Carpet Factory.[@woven-wilton-cutting-wire]

Brussels pile is looped; Wilton has the loops cut. In the 1842 *Britannica*'s account, long steel wires were inserted between the linen and worsted warps of a Brussels loom, and the worsted pile looped up over them; Brussels and Wilton were woven "in looms of the same construction". The National Park Service adds that Brussels was woven in strips on a 27-inch loom.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop] Wilton's wires had a groove along which a knife, the "travat", was run, cutting the loops to give a velvet surface. Merriam-Webster dates the first known use of "Wilton" for the carpet to 1774.[@woven-wilton-cutting-wire]

When the cutting began is disputed. All accounts credit the 9th Earl of Pembroke with bringing French weavers to Wilton, smuggled in wine barrels in one legend; the town's heritage trail says 1741, the firm Wilton Carpets 1721.[@woven-wilton-french-weavers] The Victoria County History gives the first written evidence of carpet at Wilton as a patent of 1741 to Ignatius Couran, John Barford and William Moody for carpeting "known as Moccadoes". It notes that the patented loom had no device for cutting the loops, and points to French moquettes as their likelier origin; the firm credits a weaver it calls Defosse with the cutting.[@woven-wilton-1741-patent-moccadoes; @wilton-1741]

Kidderminster set up a Brussels loom in 1749. Tradition says John Broom "travelled to Brussels, Tournai, or Wilton" and came back with the plans and the workers; the local-history site that tells it warns that none of the versions can be proved and all suggest industrial espionage. The two towns became rivals.[@woven-kidderminster-brussels-1749; @woven-wilton-1741-patent-moccadoes] By the time of Christie's sale in 1800, all four woven kinds were stock in trade, and they were also crossing the Atlantic.

## Dye notebooks from Tariffville, Connecticut, about 1840

The Winterthur Library holds two notebooks of recipes for dyeing wool for Brussels carpets at the mills of the Tariff Manufacturing Company, from about 1840. The mills closed in 1867 after a fire.[@wint-lib-tariff-brussels] The notebooks show an American mill dyeing yarn for the loom's pile carpet, and they come late in a story that began with imports.

The National Park Service finds Brussels and Wilton in American inventories and advertisements from the 1750s.[@nps-wilton-america] But the Park Service also says that by the last quarter of the century most middle-class houses had inexpensive Scotch carpeting, floorcloths or straw mats, and it quotes Tobias Lear, writing for George Washington in 1790: "Scotch Carpeting is almost the only kind to be found here in New York".[@woven-scotch-carpet-america-1790]

American weaving started at the dear end and then moved to the cheap one. By the Park Service's account, America's first full commercial carpet factory, William Peter Sprague's in Philadelphia (1791), knotted carpet in the Axminster manner. Ingrain came to American mills later: the Tariff Manufacturing Company at Simsbury, Connecticut, incorporated in 1825, and Orrin Thompson's works at Thompsonville, founded in 1828, which made "inexpensive flat-woven ingrain carpeting" and became Bigelow-Hartford.[@woven-us-ingrain-1820s] An ingrain fragment at Historic New England keeps both its selvages, showing the narrow loom width that was sewn into room-size carpet.[@hne-1959-7b-ingrain] The next change came to the loom itself.

## Bigelow's patent model, 1838

<Figure id="bigelow-mill" size="margin" caption="The Bigelow Carpet Mill at Clinton, Massachusetts, where the power Brussels loom went into production." />

The Smithsonian holds Erastus Bigelow's patent model of a loom, dated 1838.[@si-bigelow-loom-model] It came after three decades of change in how carpet looms made pattern. On the draw loom, a second worker, the drawboy, selected the warp ends for each pattern pick. Britannica dates Joseph-Marie Jacquard's punched-card attachment to 1804–05 (another account says 1801), and a Worcester local history says it reached Kidderminster by 1825. Another account says it replaced the "mounture" of the Brussels loom and was hard for small loom shops to afford, hastening the move out of the cottages.[@drawloom-to-jacquard-carpet] The older method survives on paper: Michelle McFadden's 1993 Winterthur Program thesis studies 46 watercolour drafts for Brussels or Wilton carpet, signed and dated by I. Arbuthnot in 1803–06 and 1813–17, which, according to its abstract, hold information on the draw-loom weaving process. Where the drafts are now is not yet known.[@thesis-mcfadden-arbuthnot-drafts]

Bigelow went further and drove the loom by power. He built power looms for ingrain about 1839–41 (sources vary between 1839, 1841 and 1843) and then for Brussels and Wilton, between 1845 and 1851. Accounts tie them to cheaper production, with lower-paid women and boys in place of male weavers.[@bigelow-power-loom] The power Brussels loom went into production at the Bigelow mill in Clinton, Massachusetts, which dates from 1848.[@bigelow-mill]

The knotted carpet's name then returned on a machine. Halcyon Skinner is credited with perfecting the power loom for "Royal Axminster" in 1876, for Alexander Smith & Sons of Yonkers, a claim still to be corroborated.[@skinner-axminster-1876] The machine Axminster only borrowed the name of Whitty's hand-knotted carpet.[@axminster-whitty-1755] A spool Axminster loom photographed at Shaw Industries in Dalton carries tube frames overhead, each wound with one row of coloured pile yarn; the loom inserts a row of tufts from one spool, cuts them and weaves them in. The identification rests on the photograph alone.[@own-spool-axminster-loom] Those were the looms tufting would meet.

## Mohasco's annual report for 1957

<Figure id="u-mvs3lacgfbrui" size="margin" caption="Cover of Mohasco Industries’ annual report for 1957, a 19-page pamphlet. Hagley Museum and Library." />

The Hagley Museum and Library holds the 1957 annual report of Mohasco Industries, a pamphlet of 19 pages. Under "Products" it reports new manufacturing techniques for the company's "tufted and velvet fabric lines", perfected during the year, with patents received or pending.[@u-mvs3lacgfbrui, page image 7] Behind Mohasco stood old weaving firms. Mohawk's founders, the Shuttleworths, came from England in 1875 and wove carpet at Amsterdam, New York, on secondhand looms; a merger of 1920 made Mohawk Carpet Mills.[@co-mohawk] Its 1956 merger with Alexander Smith made Mohasco, as chapter 6 tells.[@mohasco-1956]

By one account the woven firms were already weak: carpet sales fell in the mid-1920s and firms such as Bigelow and Mohawk struggled before the Depression.[@woven-slump-1920s] The rival was cheaper: tufted carpet reportedly sold wholesale at about half the price of woven,[@tufted-half-price] made, according to the Carpet and Rug Institute, on machinery developed for chenille rugs and then widened to make broadloom (chapter 4).[@chenille-widened] Mohasco then moved south, to a tufted plant at Dublin, Georgia; its weaving of Wilton and velvet at Laurens, South Carolina, had ended by 1965, and carpet making at Amsterdam ended about 1968.[@co-mohawk] Langhorne Carpet in Pennsylvania, noted in the research for this book as one of the last American Wilton mills, has since closed; the date and a published source are still to be traced.[@langhorne-wilton]

This chapter argues that tufting entered a market the loom had already made. Knotted carpet stayed costly, and from the 1730s British and then American looms supplied the rest of the market, from flat Scotch cloth to Wilton pile, with power looms making it cheaper again. It was this woven industry that tufted carpet, reportedly at about half its wholesale price, undercut.
