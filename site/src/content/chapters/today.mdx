A photograph taken at Shaw Industries in Dalton shows a green cast-iron carpet loom. Chains of tube frames hang overhead, each spool wound with one row of coloured pile yarn; the loom takes one spool at a time, inserts a row of tufts, cuts them and weaves them in, and the carpet on its cloth beam carries a many-coloured pattern. Identified so far only from the photograph, it is a spool Axminster loom, a type that dates from Halcyon Skinner's loom of 1876.[@own-spool-axminster-loom] It is a weaving machine in the keeping of a firm that did not begin as a carpet maker at all. Shaw started in 1946 as Star Dye Co., dyeing small tufted goods, bedspreads and scatter rugs, during Dalton's chenille boom.[@co-shaw]

<Figure id="own-spool-axminster-loom" size="wide" caption="A spool Axminster loom photographed at Shaw Industries, Dalton: chains of pre-wound yarn spools above, patterned carpet on the beam, and a wall of yarn cones behind." />

The firms that tuft today mostly came to it the same way, from another trade, and most of them have since been merged, bought or divided. Nearly everything known about them comes from their own websites and from summaries of trade and local histories, so their claims are reported here as theirs.

## Dalton's newcomers

Shaw's road to carpet ran through finishing. As Star Finishing Co. it became, by the company's account, the world's largest commission finisher of tufted carpet; Shaw dates that name to 1958, the Georgia Encyclopedia to 1960. Its first move into making carpet came when a holding company formed in 1967 bought the Philadelphia Carpet Co., founded in 1846. It went public as Shaw Industries in 1971 and opened its first yarn plant in 1972.[@co-shaw] Randall Patton has written its history.[@book-patton-shaw]

Its neighbours came from yarn, sales and other textiles. The Dixie Group was founded in 1920 as Dixie Mercerizing Co., treating cotton yarn for hosiery. The company dates its entry into carpet to 1951, when it bought Dalton Candlewick, a specialty yarn maker; it became Dixie Yarns in 1965 as it moved into high-volume tufting yarns, and made carpet itself only from 1993, when it bought Carriage Industries and Masland Carpets.[@co-dixie-group] World Carpets was founded in Dalton in 1954 by Shaheen Shaheen, a former chenille-company salesman, and his wife Piera. Biographies of Shaheen credit it with being one of the first carpet firms to integrate from spinning to delivery and the first to run a Küster continuous dyeing range, in 1968.[@co-world-carpets] Milliken, a textile company founded in 1865, entered carpet in 1968 by buying Callaway Mills of LaGrange, Georgia.[@co-milliken] Ray Anderson left Milliken in 1973 to bring European free-lay carpet tiles to the United States, in a joint venture with the British firm Carpets International; the company that became Interface opened its first plant at LaGrange in 1973, or 1974 in encyclopedia accounts.[@co-interface] Even the newest of the large Dalton tufters is a departure: Bob Shaw, former chief executive of Shaw Industries, founded Engineered Floors and broke ground on a plant at Calhoun in 2009, built around solution-dyed polyester.[@co-engineered-floors]

The one old weaver among the Georgia giants is Mohawk. The Shuttleworth family wove carpet in Amsterdam, New York, from 1878, on secondhand looms; a merger in 1920 formed Mohawk Carpet Mills, and the merger with Alexander Smith in 1956 formed Mohasco. Mohasco moved south. By 1965 it had a tufted plant at Dublin, Georgia, and had ended Wilton and velvet weaving at Laurens, South Carolina; carpet making in Amsterdam ended about 1968. When Mohasco first tufted is still unknown, and 1965 is the earliest date found.[@co-mohawk]

## Weavers who came late

The first tufters abroad were newcomers too. In September 1959 a German trade journal described tufting as practised "bisher nur durch Außenseiter", so far only by outsiders.[@ner-de-outsiders-1959] In Japan, Nihon Jūtan installed its first Wilton loom in 1955 but a tufting machine only in 1981.[@ner-jp-slow-diffusion] The pattern has exceptions: Japan's first tufting firm, Suminoe, was an established carpet weaver.[@ner-outsiders] Chapter 7 follows those first adopters.

The European and Australian mills in this chapter's records belong to the later wave, weavers and woollen mills that took up tufting in the 1960s and 1970s. Ege began at Herning, Denmark, in 1938 with two looms, registered first as a cotton mill; the company says it built a 25,000 m² tufting factory there in the early 1970s.[@co-ege] Balta was set up in Belgium in 1964 to make woven area rugs, and its Imperial Tufting Company followed at Tielt in 1974.[@co-balta] Godfrey Hirst traces itself to a woollen mill set up at Geelong in 1865 and made blankets, rugs, tweeds and flannel until it narrowed to carpet in 1966; whether that carpet was tufted from the start has not been found.[@co-godfrey-hirst] Victoria Carpets was founded at Kirkcaldy in 1895 and moved in 1900 to Kidderminster, the centre of British carpet weaving; it now makes tufted as well as woven broadloom, and carpet tiles.[@co-victoria] Desso, according to its owner Tarkett, began at Oss as a maker of woven carpets, and is now known for commercial carpet tiles and rolls.[@co-desso] In Egypt, Oriental Weavers began with a single loom in 1979 and remains mainly a maker of machine-woven rugs; its tufted arm, MAC Carpet, was established in 1981.[@co-oriental-weavers]

For most of these firms the year they began to tuft is either the company's own date or not recorded at all. No date has been found for Desso, for Kasthall, or for the Vorwerk carpet mill, which in a company interview names tufting among its techniques.[@co-desso; @co-kasthall; @co-vorwerk] Some weavers never made the change: Merinos of Gaziantep, which says it makes about 40 million m² of machine-made rugs a year, is a weaver, not a tufter.[@co-merinos]

Tufting also flowed back to Dalton from Europe. Roger De Clerck began in 1959 with an upholstery-fabric workshop on the family farm in West Flanders (one biography calls it a carpet company), and in 1978 his daughter Mieke De Clerck and son-in-law Carl Bouckaert founded Beaulieu of America, which became a large Dalton maker of tufted carpet.[@co-beaulieu]

## Bought, merged and divided

The second pattern in the records is consolidation. Mohawk merged with World Carpets in November 1998 and acquired Godfrey Hirst in 2018.[@co-world-carpets; @co-godfrey-hirst] Shaw bought West Point-Pepperell's carpet business in 1987, Salem in 1992 and Queen in 1999, and has been owned by Berkshire Hathaway since 2001.[@co-shaw] Beaulieu of America went bankrupt in 2017 and its assets went to Engineered Floors, which had also bought Dream Weaver Carpet and merged with J+J Flooring; by 2019 Engineered Floors had about 12 plants, all in the Dalton area.[@co-beaulieu; @co-engineered-floors] Interface's acquisitions included Heuga, in 1988.[@co-interface]

Europe followed the same course. Desso passed through owners including DLW, Armstrong and NPM Capital before Tarkett agreed to buy it on 30 October 2014.[@co-desso] From 2012 Geoff Wilding turned Victoria into an acquisitive flooring group, adding Victoria Carpets Australia in 2014, Abingdon Flooring and, in 2022, Balta's rugs and residential businesses; what remained of the Balta group was renamed Belysse.[@co-victoria; @co-balta] Object Carpet, a German contract tufter founded in 1972, absorbed its sister firm Toucan-T in 2021.[@co-object-carpet]

New mills are still being built by firms from outside carpet. The home-textile maker Welspun India formed Welspun Flooring in March 2016, and its plant at Hyderabad, making carpet tiles and broadloom among other floorings, was under construction in 2018 for a start in 2019.[@co-welspun-flooring] Huade Carpet Group of Zhengzhou says it makes machine-tufted carpet on machinery from Germany, Britain and Belgium, though its own pages give both 1986 and 1997 as its start.[@co-huade]

The size of these groups is usually stated in their own words. Victoria describes itself as the UK's largest carpet manufacturer, and Welspun calls its plant "Asia's largest".[@co-victoria; @co-welspun-flooring] Mohawk's title of the world's largest flooring manufacturer counts all flooring by revenue, not carpet, and Shaw's claim to be the world's largest carpet manufacturer dates from 1987.[@co-mohawk; @co-shaw] These are claims, not measurements, and this chapter does not rank the firms.

## Studios with borrowed beginnings

The custom studios have their own founding stories, and they too have been gathered up. In the United States two names come first. Stanislav V'Soske and his brothers set up a carpet workshop in Grand Rapids, Michigan, which a local history dates to 1920. Trade sources credit Stanislav with developing "the modern hand-tufting technique" in 1924, a claim to treat with care, since hand-tufting tools were being patented in the United States in the same years.[@co-vsoske] In 1941 the Museum of Modern Art commissioned V'Soske to make rugs from designs by American artists; Stuart Davis's "Flying Carpet" and Arshile Gorky's "Bull in the Sun" are in its collection.[@co-vsoske] Edward Fields founded his New York carpet house in 1935. The company says he developed a hand-tufting tool, the "Magic Needle", in the mid-1930s, and that he coined the term "area rug", which its pages tie both to a 1952 series of five rugs by Raymond Loewy and to a first collection at Lord & Taylor in the 1950s.[@co-edward-fields] Their tools belong to chapter 9.

Neither firm stands alone now. Jack Fields sold Edward Fields to Tai Ping in 2005, and it operates as a Tai Ping brand.[@co-edward-fields] V'Soske's line continues in PWV Studios, a hand-tufting studio founded by V'Soske in 1983, which belongs to the Scott Group of Grand Rapids, as does the hand-knotting firm Hokanson.[@co-scott-group; @co-vsoske] Scott itself began in 1969 as Scott Carpet Mills in Cleveland. It made the Oval Office rug of 2010 and says it has more than 50 tufting artisans in Grand Rapids.[@co-scott-group]

The later studios also came to tufting from somewhere else. Pinton, a tapestry manufacture at Felletin near Aubusson founded in 1867, says it was "one of the first manufactories" to use gun tufting and has specialised in hand-tufted rugs since the early 1990s. Lucas Pinton took it over in 2002, when it was near bankruptcy, and in May 2024 it opened an 1,800 m² tufting plant at Croze.[@co-pinton] Kasthall, founded in 1889 and making its rugs at Kinna in Sweden since 1901, describes its tufted rugs as wool or linen punched through a backing with a tufting tool.[@co-kasthall] Limited Edition, founded at Mouscron in Belgium in 1991, began with sisal rug borders and, by its founder's account, went on to buy struggling weaving and tufting companies.[@co-limited-edition] In County Wexford, according to the Irish Times, Denis Kenny and Fiona Gilboy took over in 1981 a small hearth-rug business founded by Tom Ceadogán, a dyehouse master; other sources date Ceadogán Rugmakers to 1989.[@co-ceadogan] Designer Rugs was started in Sydney in 1986 by a family with no prior rug experience, to offer designs that imported rugs lacked.[@co-designer-rugs] Roger Oates, founded in 1989 and best known for flatweave runners, makes its hand-tufted rugs in a company workshop in Devon.[@co-roger-oates] Creative Accents in California dates itself to 1967 and tells how Ken Hensler made "one of the world's first shag rugs" on his wife's sewing machine in the early 1960s; both are the company's claims.[@co-creative-accents]

Kramis, at Altbüron near Lucerne, founded in 1987, is the plainest case of the small studio. It makes only bespoke hand-tufted rugs with single-needle tufting guns, with yarn, tufting and finishing on one site, and *Monocle* reported in 2011 that about 12 staff made up to 4 carpets a day. A tufting video it posted on Instagram in 2022 brought it international attention, a link to the hobby revival (chapter 13).[@co-kramis]

## Hand and machine under one roof

The line between hand and machine tufting now runs through firms rather than between them. Tai Ping was founded in Hong Kong in 1956 by Sir Lawrence and Sir Horace Kadoorie with partners, to keep Chinese carpet craft alive and employ refugees; its early breakthrough was a switch from hand-knotting to hand-tufting, and it later made machine-tufted carpet as well.[@co-tai-ping] Jaipur Living, started in Rajasthan in 1978 with 2 looms and 9 artisans, is a hand-knotting business at its core, but it also makes hand-tufted rugs, gun-tufted through a pre-woven cloth with a cotton-latex backing, and power-loomed lines.[@co-jaipur] Kaleen India, founded in 1996, makes hand-knotted, hand-tufted, flatweave and power-loom rugs in plants at Panipat, Mirzapur and Bikaner.[@co-kaleen-india] Both Jaipur Living and Surya, which began in 1976 as a small supplier of hand-knotted rugs at Ugapur, now run their American business from Georgia, Jaipur from Atlanta and Surya from Cartersville.[@co-jaipur; @co-surya] Chapter 10 follows the Indian side.

Delos, founded in 2005 by Ivan and Leah Phillips at Calhoun, Georgia, shows the division inside one company. Its "American Made" rugs are tufted by robotic or CNC machines in Georgia and finished by hand; its "Journey Made" rugs are hand-tufted or woven overseas. It added an aircraft-carpet division in 2013, and in 2021 bought Masland's US custom area-rug equipment and added tufting machines.[@co-delos] Chapter 12 follows the machines.

<Figure id="pat-us5090341a" size="margin" caption="William H. Satterfield, “Hand-held Tufting Mending Gun”, US patent 5,090,341, filed 1990 and granted 1992." />

The hand tool has a place even in the broadloom mill. In conversation with Tim Eads, Shaw Industries' design team said that hand-held tufting guns are still used on the broadloom line to fix mistakes found at final inspection; a written or photographed source for the practice is still to be found.[@finding-shaw-mending-gun] A Dalton patent describes such a tool: William H. Satterfield's "Hand-held Tufting Mending Gun", filed in 1990 and assigned to Spencer Wright Industries.[@pat-us5090341a]

This chapter argues that the firms that tuft today mostly came to tufting from another trade, whether dyeing, yarn, weaving or tapestry, and that mergers have since gathered mills and studios alike into fewer groups, inside which hand and machine tufting often work side by side.
