A photograph taken at Shaw Industries in Dalton shows a green cast-iron carpet loom. Chains of tube frames hang overhead, each spool wound with one row of coloured pile yarn; the loom takes one spool at a time, inserts a row of tufts, cuts them and weaves them in, and the carpet on its cloth beam carries a many-coloured pattern. Identified so far only from the photograph, it appears to be a spool Axminster loom, a type that dates from Halcyon Skinner's loom of 1876.[@own-spool-axminster-loom] It is a weaving machine in the keeping of a firm that did not begin as a carpet maker at all.

Most of the firms traced here came to tufting from another trade, and many of them have since merged, bought others or been bought. Nearly everything known about them comes from their own websites and from trade and local histories, most of them read so far only in search-result summaries, so their claims are reported here as theirs.

## Star Dye Co. and its Dalton neighbours

Shaw started in 1946 as Star Dye Co., dyeing small tufted goods, bedspreads and scatter rugs, during Dalton's chenille boom. As Star Finishing Co. it became, by the company's account, the world's largest commission finisher of tufted carpet; Shaw dates that name to 1958, the Georgia Encyclopedia to 1960. It first made carpet when a holding company formed in 1967 bought the Philadelphia Carpet Co., founded in 1846.[@co-shaw]

Its neighbours came from yarn, sales and other textiles. The Dixie Group began in 1920 as Dixie Mercerizing Co., treating cotton yarn for hosiery, and made carpet itself only from 1993, when it bought Carriage Industries and Masland Carpets.[@co-dixie-group] World Carpets was founded in Dalton in 1954 by Shaheen Shaheen, a former chenille-company salesman, and his wife Piera.[@co-world-carpets] Milliken, a textile company founded in 1865, entered carpet in 1968 by buying Callaway Mills of LaGrange, Georgia; Ray Anderson left Milliken in 1973 to bring European carpet tiles to the United States, founding the firm that became Interface.[@co-milliken; @co-interface] Engineered Floors, which broke ground at Calhoun in 2009, was founded by Bob Shaw, former chief executive of Shaw Industries.[@co-engineered-floors]

The old weaver among the Georgia giants is Mohawk. The Shuttleworth family wove carpet in Amsterdam, New York, from 1878; a merger in 1920 formed Mohawk Carpet Mills, and the merger with Alexander Smith in 1956 formed Mohasco. By 1965 Mohasco had a tufted plant at Dublin, Georgia, and had ended Wilton and velvet weaving at Laurens, South Carolina; carpet making in Amsterdam ended about 1968.[@co-mohawk] In Europe, too, weavers came to tufting, some of them late.

## Ege's tufting factory at Herning

Ege began at Herning, Denmark, in 1938 with two looms, registered first as a cotton mill. The company says it built a 25,000 m² tufting factory there in the early 1970s.[@co-ege] Elsewhere the first to tuft were sometimes outsiders, and some weavers came late. In September 1959 a German trade journal described tufting as practised "bisher nur durch Außenseiter", so far only by outsiders.[@ner-de-outsiders-1959; @ner-outsiders] In Japan, Nihon Jūtan installed its first Wilton loom in 1955 but a tufting machine only in 1981.[@ner-jp-slow-diffusion] The pattern has exceptions: Japan's first tufter, Suminoe, was an established carpet weaver.[@ner-outsiders] Chapter 7 follows those first adopters.

Several other mills in this chapter's records were weavers or woollen mills before they tufted. Balta was set up in Belgium in 1964 to make woven area rugs, and its Imperial Tufting Company followed at Tielt in 1974.[@co-balta] Godfrey Hirst traces itself to a woollen mill set up at Geelong in 1865 and made blankets, rugs, tweeds and flannel until it narrowed to carpet in 1966; whether that carpet was tufted from the start has not been found.[@co-godfrey-hirst] In Egypt, Oriental Weavers began with a single loom in 1979 (1980 in one account) and remains mainly a maker of machine-woven rugs; its tufted arm, MAC Carpet, was established in 1981.[@co-oriental-weavers]

For many of these firms the year they began to tuft is the company's own date or is not recorded at all. The Swedish firm Kasthall, founded in 1889 and making its rugs at Kinna since 1901, makes woven and hand-tufted rugs to order, but when it began to tuft has not been found.[@co-kasthall] No date has been found either for Desso, which according to its owner Tarkett began at Oss as a maker of woven carpets, or for the Vorwerk carpet mill, which in a company interview names tufting among its techniques.[@co-desso; @co-vorwerk]

## Victoria's move to Kidderminster

<Figure id="co-victoria" caption="Victoria Carpets, founded at Kirkcaldy in 1895, moved to Kidderminster in 1900." />

Victoria Carpets was founded at Kirkcaldy in 1895 and moved in 1900 to Kidderminster, the centre of British carpet weaving; it now makes tufted as well as woven carpet.[@co-victoria] Its later history shows the second pattern in these records, consolidation. From 2012 Geoff Wilding turned Victoria into an acquisitive flooring group, adding Victoria Carpets Australia in 2014, Abingdon Flooring and, in 2022, Balta's rugs and residential businesses; what remained of the Balta group was renamed Belysse.[@co-victoria; @co-balta]

Dalton's firms were gathered up in the same way. Mohawk merged with World Carpets in November 1998 and acquired Godfrey Hirst in 2018.[@co-world-carpets; @co-godfrey-hirst] Shaw bought West Point-Pepperell's carpet business in 1987, Salem in 1992 and Queen in 1999, and has been owned by Berkshire Hathaway since 2001.[@co-shaw] Beaulieu of America was founded in 1978 by Mieke De Clerck and Carl Bouckaert, daughter and son-in-law of Roger De Clerck, who had begun in 1959 with an upholstery-fabric workshop on the family farm in West Flanders (one biography calls it a carpet company). It became a large Dalton maker of tufted carpet, went bankrupt in 2017, and its assets went to Engineered Floors, which also bought Dream Weaver Carpet and merged with J+J Flooring; by 2019 Engineered Floors had about 12 plants, all in the Dalton area.[@co-beaulieu; @co-engineered-floors] Desso passed through owners including DLW, Armstrong and NPM Capital before Tarkett agreed to buy it on 30 October 2014.[@co-desso]

The size of these groups is usually stated in their own words. Victoria describes itself as the UK's largest carpet manufacturer and Welspun calls its Indian plant "Asia's largest", while the description of Mohawk as the world's largest flooring manufacturer counts all flooring by revenue, not carpet.[@co-victoria; @co-welspun-flooring; @co-mohawk] These are claims, not measurements, and this chapter does not rank the firms. The custom studios have been gathered up too.

## Edward Fields's "Magic Needle"

<Figure id="co-edward-fields" caption="Edward Fields, the New York carpet house founded in 1935, now a Tai Ping brand." />

Edward Fields and his wife founded their New York carpet house in 1935. The company says he developed a hand-tufting tool, the "Magic Needle", in the mid-1930s, and that he coined the term "area rug", which its pages tie to rugs of the 1950s.[@co-edward-fields] The other early American name is V'Soske. Stanislav V'Soske and his brothers set up a carpet workshop in Grand Rapids, Michigan, which a local history dates to 1920, and trade sources credit Stanislav with developing "the modern hand-tufting technique" in 1924. The claim needs care, since hand-tufting tools were being patented in the United States in the same years.[@co-vsoske] Their tools belong to chapter 9.

Neither firm stands alone now. Jack Fields sold Edward Fields to Tai Ping in 2005, and it operates as a Tai Ping brand.[@co-edward-fields] V'Soske's line continues in PWV Studios, a hand-tufting studio founded by V'Soske in 1983, which belongs to the Scott Group of Grand Rapids, as does the hand-knotting firm Hokanson.[@co-scott-group; @co-vsoske] Scott itself began in 1969 as Scott Carpet Mills in Cleveland. It made the Oval Office rug of 2010 and says it has more than 50 tufting artisans in Grand Rapids.[@co-scott-group] Some studios, like the mills, came to tufting from an older trade.

## Pinton's workshops at Felletin

<Figure id="co-pinton" caption="Pinton, the tapestry manufacture founded at Felletin, near Aubusson, in 1867." />

Pinton is a tapestry manufacture at Felletin near Aubusson, founded in 1867. It says it was "one of the first manufactories" to use gun tufting and has specialised in hand-tufted rugs since the early 1990s, and in May 2024 it opened an 1,800 m² tufting plant at Croze.[@co-pinton] Other studios began from rug borders, a hearth-rug business or no rug experience at all. Limited Edition, founded in 1991 at Mouscron in Belgium, began with sisal rug borders and, by its founder's account, went on to buy struggling weaving and tufting companies.[@co-limited-edition] In County Wexford, according to the Irish Times, Denis Kenny and Fiona Gilboy took over in 1981 a small hearth-rug business founded by Tom Ceadogán, a dyehouse master; other sources date Ceadogán Rugmakers to 1989.[@co-ceadogan] Designer Rugs was started in Sydney in 1986 by a family with no prior rug experience, to offer designs that imported rugs lacked.[@co-designer-rugs] Creative Accents in California dates itself to 1967 and tells how Ken Hensler made "one of the world's first shag rugs" on his wife's sewing machine in the early 1960s; both are the company's claims.[@co-creative-accents]

Kramis, founded in 1987 at Altbüron in the canton of Lucerne, is the plainest case of the small studio. It makes only bespoke hand-tufted rugs with single-needle tufting guns, and *Monocle* reported in 2011 that about 12 staff made up to 4 carpets a day. An Instagram tufting video in 2022 brought it international attention, a link to the hobby revival (chapter 13).[@co-kramis] Other firms keep hand and machine work under one roof.

## Delos at Calhoun

The line between hand and machine work now runs through firms rather than between them. Delos, founded in 2005 by Ivan and Leah Phillips and based at Calhoun, Georgia, shows it most plainly. Its "American Made" rugs are tufted by robotic or CNC machines in Georgia and finished by hand; its "Journey Made" rugs are hand-tufted or woven overseas. It added an aircraft-carpet division in 2013, and in 2021 bought Masland's US custom area-rug equipment and added tufting machines.[@co-delos] Chapter 12 follows the machines.

Tai Ping was founded in Hong Kong in 1956 by Sir Lawrence and Sir Horace Kadoorie with partners, to keep Chinese carpet craft alive and employ refugees; by its own account its early breakthrough was a switch from hand-knotting to hand-tufting, and it later made machine-tufted carpet as well.[@co-tai-ping] Jaipur Living, started in Rajasthan in 1978 with 2 looms and 9 artisans, is a hand-knotting business at its core, but it also makes hand-tufted rugs and power-loomed lines.[@co-jaipur] Both Jaipur Living and Surya, which began in 1976 as a small supplier of hand-knotted rugs at Ugapur, now run their American business from Georgia, Jaipur from Atlanta and Surya from Cartersville.[@co-jaipur; @co-surya] Chapter 10 follows the Indian side. The hand tool, meanwhile, has kept a place inside the broadloom mill itself.

## A mending gun from Dalton, 1990

<Figure id="pat-us5090341a" size="margin" caption="William H. Satterfield, “Hand-held Tufting Mending Gun”, US patent 5,090,341, filed 1990 and granted 1992." />

A Dalton patent filed in 1990, William H. Satterfield's "Hand-held Tufting Mending Gun", assigned to Spencer Wright Industries, describes a hand-held gun for mending tufted carpet.[@pat-us5090341a] In conversation with Tim Eads, Shaw Industries' design team said that hand-held tufting guns are still used on the broadloom line to fix mistakes found at final inspection; a written or photographed source for the practice is still to be found.[@finding-shaw-mending-gun] If so, the firm that keeps a woven-carpet loom in Dalton also mends its machine-tufted broadloom with a hand gun.

This chapter argues that most of the firms traced here that tuft today came to tufting from another trade, whether dyeing, yarn, weaving or tapestry, and that mergers have since gathered mills and studios alike into fewer groups, inside which hand and machine production often sit side by side.
