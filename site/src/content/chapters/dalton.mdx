Near Dalton, Georgia, in 1895, a fifteen-year-old named Catherine Evans sewed thick yarn into muslin and shrank the cloth to lock the tufts. The result was a candlewick bedspread (chapter 2), and she kept it.[@event-whitener-bedspread; @ttw-evans-1895-1900] Her first sale came in 1900, for $2.50, to her brother's new sister-in-law. The two dates should not be merged into one.[@ttw-evans-1895-1900] This chapter follows that one spread into a trade, and the trade into an industry whose machines went on to make carpet, by way of things it left behind.

## A photograph on U.S. Highway 41, 1933

<Figure id="photo-green-burton-1933" size="wide" caption="Mrs. J. A. Green and her son Allen Burton tufting bedspreads beside U.S. Highway 41, 1933." />

A photograph of 1933, now in the Georgia Archives' Vanishing Georgia collection, shows Mrs. J. A. Green and her son, Allen Burton, tufting bedspreads beside U.S. Highway 41 in Bartow County.[@photo-green-burton-1933] By then Evans's craft had become a trade. The spreads were tufted by hand and sold at the roadside, and this highway took the nickname Peacock Alley from the peacock motif.[@article-nge-chenille; @own-bandy-peacock-spreads]

How much the workers earned is uncertain. One account puts the pay of the "turfers" at 25 to 50 cents a spread, while Sears sold the spreads for $1.59 to $2.49 as "handmade by the southern mountain women"; the figures are still to be traced to their source.[@ttw-turfer-pay] The word "turfing" was older than the trade: in 1891 Ebenezer Ross of Toledo, Ohio, had patented a two-handled needle-and-gauge tool as a "Turfing Implement" (chapter 9).[@pat-us463548a]

Four years after the photograph was taken, Catherine Evans, by then Mrs. Whitener, told her own story in the *Dalton News* of 11 March 1937, under the headline "Mrs. Whitener Recalls Beginning of Bedspread Industry". It is a first-hand account from forty years on, and it has not yet been seen for this book.[@article-whitener-1937-dalton-news] A scholarly chapter on her life appears in *Georgia Women: Their Lives and Times* (2014).[@book-georgia-women-whitener-2014] Spreads with the roadside's motif can still be seen in Dalton.

## Two peacock spreads in a Dalton gallery

<Pair>
  <Figure id="own-bandy-peacock-spreads" image={1} caption="Fan-tailed peacock in rows of separate cut tufts on a yellow ground, with a scalloped tufted edge." />
  <Figure id="own-bandy-peacock-spreads" caption="Standing pink peacock: solid areas in packed rows of tufts, outlines in single rows." />
</Pair>

The Shaw Floors / Bandy art center on South Depot Street in Dalton has two spreads with the bird that named the highway. On a yellow ground, a fan-tailed peacock perches on a branch, worked almost entirely in rows of separate cut tufts in blue, red, pink, green, brown and white, inside a scalloped tufted edge. On a white ground, a standing pink peacock carries a dense tufted tail with blue "eyes", framed by green and pink foliage.[@own-bandy-peacock-spreads]

The two birds show how a spread is drawn. Separate tufts make dots, a single row of tufts set close together makes a line, and packed rows fill a shape: the bodies are solid with rows, the outlines are single rows.[@own-bandy-peacock-spreads] Other spreads at the centre show the two looks, with continuous rows of cut pile drawing tulips and scrolls in two greens, and separate tufts stippling a red and tan floral inside a diamond border.[@own-bandy-chenille-spreads] The same needle and cutter give either a drawn line or a stippled dot.

The makers and dates of these spreads have not yet been recorded; peacock spreads are associated with the roadside trade of the 1930s to the 1950s.[@own-bandy-peacock-spreads; @own-bandy-chenille-spreads] Beyond the art center, the Bandy Heritage Center at Dalton State College keeps the Sandra Mahs collection of chenille bedspreads from the cottage-industry years,[@bandy-mahs-chenille] and Ashley Callahan's *Southern Tufts* (2015) follows the same yarn into chenille clothing and its national craze.[@book-callahan-southern-tufts] The spreads were made for the bed, but the technique did not stay there.

## A rug for Henry Ford, 1936

<Figure id="hf-00-1510-19" size="margin" caption="Chenille rug inscribed “NEW V-8 FORD 1936”, a gift to Henry Ford." />

In 1936 someone made Henry Ford a red and white chenille rug, about 33 by 16 inches, as a thank-you gift, and inscribed it "NEW V-8 FORD 1936". It is now at The Henry Ford, though not on exhibit.[@hf-00-1510-19] Its maker is not named, but it is a dated, homemade tufted rug from the hand era, and it is a rug: the spread-maker's technique had reached the floor.[@hf-00-1510-19]

In December of the same year a Dalton man filed a patent for a machine to do the work.[@event-looper-tufting-machine]

## Kile's patent drawing for Cabin Crafts, 1939

<Figure id="pat-us2389914a" size="margin" caption="Jess Spencer Kile, “Machine and Method for Making Tufted Articles”, filed 1939 for Cabin Crafts." />

The Dalton man was Glenn Looper. According to the Carpet and Rug Institute, he adapted a Singer sewing machine for tufting; his patent for a "Tufting Machine" was filed in December 1936 and granted in 1938.[@event-looper-tufting-machine] His machine and its rivals are the subject of chapter 5. The drawing here belongs to a later Dalton filing. On 25 July 1939 Jess Spencer Kile applied for a patent on a "Machine and Method for Making Tufted Articles", assigned to Cabin Crafts and granted in 1945.[@pat-us2389914a]

Cabin Crafts, founded by the Westcott brothers, is described in sources so far seen only in search results as one of the firms that moved spread-making from the hand to the machine.[@co-cabin-crafts] A historical marker on the mechanization of the bedspread industry names Looper, and also Joe Cobble and George Muse, whose joint patent for a "Multiple Needle Tufting Machine" was filed in 1941 and granted in 1943.[@marker-mechanization-bedspread; @pat-us2335487a]

The change was fast. By one count, still to be traced to its source, all but about one per cent of tufted bedspreads were machine-made by 1941.[@ttw-bedspreads-1941] That April the boom reached *Reader's Digest*, in "Bedspread Bonanza" by R. E. Hamilton, later executive secretary of the Tufted Textile Manufacturers Association.[@article-hamilton-1941-readers-digest] A photograph at the Bandy Heritage Center shows Cabin Crafts executives R. G. McCamy and Fred L. Westcott with Governor Ellis Arnall and a chenille bedspread; the catalogue dates it 1962, but Arnall was governor from 1943 to 1947, so the date is still to be settled.[@photo-cabin-crafts-arnall]

The factory did not end tufting at home. After the war the machine went into the house, sold by mail.

## The Chenille Center's mail-order sheets

<Figure id="own-bandy-sims-chenille-center" caption="Sims Textile Co. mail-order sheets, with a tufted sample and a yarn tassel, at the Bandy Heritage Center." />

At the Bandy Heritage Center, three mail-order sheets from Sims Textile Co. of Dalton, "The Chenille Center", can be seen with a small tufted sample and a yarn tassel. The firm was founded in 1945, and the sheets offer everything needed to start a chenille business at home.[@own-bandy-sims-chenille-center] One shows a sewing-machine head, with its needle, presser foot and yarn tension and a stand of chenille yarn, mounted on a table with a motor, a clutch and a stop-and-start treadle.[@own-bandy-sims-chenille-center]

<PullQuote from="own-bandy-sims-chenille-center" who="Sims Textile Co., “The Chenille Center”, Dalton">Ready to plug into your light circuit and operate.</PullQuote>

As read from a photograph of the sheets, the "Electric Chenille Machine Complete With Table and Motor" cost $150, and heads alone ran from $75 for one needle to $100 for eight. Yarn was sold retail in any quantity and wholesale by the 200-pound case, with direct shipments in 75 standard colours from the mill at Carrollton, and a sheet of "Miscellaneous Information" set out the cost of making a one-needle bedspread and mentioned the highway "Spread Line".[@own-bandy-sims-chenille-center] A household could now buy a motorised machine with one needle or several by post and plug it in, a home stage that bridged hand tufting and the factory's multi-needle machine.[@own-bandy-sims-chenille-center] Sims also issued a map of Dalton about 1946, which Thomas Deaton credits to the Dalton-Whitfield County Historical Society.[@map-sims-textile-dalton-1946]

Tufting did not only make bedspreads.

## A nylon bath mat, about 1955

<Figure id="u-mvs37zoguerdx" caption="DuPont product photograph, about 1955: a bath mat and matching lid cover tufted with DuPont nylon." />

Among DuPont's product photographs at the Hagley Museum and Library is one, dated by Hagley to about 1955, of a bath mat and a matching lid cover. Hagley's description says they "are tufted with DuPont nylon".[@u-mvs37zoguerdx] Bath mats and lid covers were one more small tufted product on the road from the bed to the floor, and nylon is the subject of chapter 6.

The road ended in broadloom. According to the Carpet and Rug Institute, in words so far seen only in search results, the machinery developed for chenille rugs was widened, creating larger rugs and broadloom carpet.[@chenille-widened] Newcomers turned the machines to room-size carpet. According to sources so far seen only in search results, Eugene Barwick, who had sold tufted goods for Sears, formed Barwick Mills in 1949; one local history says its sales doubled in 1950 and passed $26 million by 1954.[@co-barwick] Dyers of small tufted goods turned to carpet too: Shaw began in 1946 as Star Dye Co., dyeing bedspreads and scatter rugs during the chenille boom (chapter 11).[@co-shaw] According to Randall Patton, tufted carpet sales across the country rose from near zero in the late 1940s to more than 100 million square yards by 1958.[@article-patton-ehnet]

The industry kept its own trade body, the Tufted Textile Manufacturers Association; a 1960 correspondence file under its name survives in the papers of E. C. Beetem and Son at Hagley.[@u-mvs3rt0be77qi] Its history has been written by Thomas Deaton in *Bedspreads to Broadloom* (1993), whose bibliography lists about 85 interviews he made between 1972 and 1993 and whose research files, with transcripts of interviews with industry pioneers, are at the Bandy Heritage Center,[@book-deaton-bedspreads-broadloom; @archive-bandy-deaton] and by Randall Patton in *Carpet Capital* (1999).[@book-patton-carpet-capital]

This chapter argues that Dalton's carpet industry grew out of a women's hand craft and a roadside market, through a stage when electric chenille machines were sold by mail for use at home, and not from the carpet mills of the North. The spreads at the art center show the needle-and-cutter technique, and the Kile patent and the Sims sheets show it passing into machines, in the factory and at home, before, by the Carpet and Rug Institute's account, those machines were widened for carpet.
