In 1949 Sergei Rudenko's excavation of Barrow 5 in the Pazyryk valley, in the Altai Mountains, reached a carpet that had been waiting in the ice. Looters had been there first and left the tomb open, and what they left behind was found frozen.[@process-knotted-pazyryk] The carpet is wool, 183 by 200 cm, with deer and mounted horsemen in its borders, and every tuft of its pile was tied by hand: a short length of yarn knotted round two warps, cut to height and locked in with wefts, one row after another.[@process-knotted-pazyryk] Its date is still argued. Radiocarbon puts it in the 5th to 4th century BCE, while the Hermitage listing dates the barrow to 252–238 BCE; either way, it is the oldest knotted-pile carpet known.[@process-knotted-pazyryk]

The same barrow held a second answer to the problem of covering a floor or a wall. Its hangings, saddlecloths and cushions were felt, cut and sewn onto felt grounds,[@process-felt-pazyryk-hangings] and felt has no yarn in it at all. Britannica describes it as wool, fur or some hair fibres locked together by heat, moisture and friction, and says that non-woven felts are considered the first textile goods produced.[@process-felt-definition]

<Figure id="process-felt-pazyryk-hangings" size="margin" caption="Fragment of felt appliqué for a carpet or wall hanging, showing a bird, from Pazyryk barrow no. 5." />

Between them, the two finds frame the question this chapter follows. A rug can do without pile, like the felt. If it has pile, like the carpet, the pile must come from somewhere, and at Pazyryk it was tied in while the cloth was being made. Tufting works the other way round: it takes a backing that already exists and puts the pile into it.[@process-tufting-overview] Following that one difference through the main ways of making a rug shows where tufting belongs.

## Rugs without pile

Felt is still made into rugs. Kyrgyz shyrdak makers cut two felts of contrasting colour at once, swap the pieces and sew them onto a coarser felt base, a craft UNESCO placed on its Urgent Safeguarding List in 2012.[@process-felt-shyrdak] The other rug without pile is woven flat. In tapestry weave each coloured weft runs back and forth only where its colour is needed and is packed down to hide the warp, and kilims, Navajo blankets, dhurries and Swedish rölakan are all made that way. Britannica traces the technique to Egyptian linen of 1483–1411 BCE from the tomb of Thutmose IV, though a 1923 account gives the tomb of Thutmose III and 1500–1400 BCE.[@process-flatweave-earliest-tapestry-egypt]

A flat weave can carry a whole scene. Where two colours meet, a kilim weaver can leave a slit, dovetail the wefts round a shared warp or interlock them,[@process-flatweave-kilim-famsf] and in India the weaver of a cotton dhurrie beats the wefts down with a pronged hand tool, the panja, until they hide the warp completely. A late 19th-century dhurrie in the Penn Museum has elephants and horses across its field; the museum says that dhurries of that time were mostly made by prison labour.[@process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn]

<Figure id="process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn" caption="Cotton dhurrie from North India, late 19th century, 232 by 137 cm, with elephants and horses." />

What a flat weave cannot give is pile. For that, the rug-maker has to tie it, raise it on a loom, or sew it in.

## Pile tied by hand

The knot travelled, and changed as it went. The Pazyryk weaver used the symmetric knot, often called Turkish or Ghiordes, which passes round two neighbouring warps and brings both ends up between them. The asymmetric knot, called Persian or Senneh, wraps fully round one warp and partly round the next, and is said to allow finer, more curving designs.[@process-knotted-knot-types] In Spain weavers tied a knot round a single warp: an armorial carpet bearing the joined arms of Alfonso V of Aragon and Maria of Castile, now in the Textile Museum, was knotted that way between 1415 and 1458.[@process-knotted-spanish-knot] In Finland the knots were spaced far apart and left long, so that the ryijy, or rya, made a shaggy, heavy cloth first used on beds; the Victoria and Albert Museum has one marked with the initials AFBD and the date 1814.[@process-knotted-rya-vam]

Tibetan weavers reach pile by another route. According to Britannica, they loop the yarn over a metal gauge rod from warp to warp and, at the end of each row, run a knife along a groove in the rod to cut the loops into pile.[@process-knotted-tibetan] Loops first and cut afterwards is the order a tufting needle follows too.[@process-tufting-overview]

Knotting was not only a village craft. A carpet attributed to Thomas Whitty of the Axminster manufactory in Devon, from about 1765, is knotted in wool by hand,[@met-928622] on the same principle as the carpet in the ice: a separate knot for each tuft.[@process-knotted-pazyryk]

## Pile raised by the loom

In Europe the next step was to make the loom form the pile itself.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop] In Brussels carpet, long steel wires are laid between the warps and the worsted pile yarn loops up over them; Wilton is the same carpet with its loops cut, made on the same narrow looms.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop; @wilton-1741]

Colour was the complication. A Brussels loom ran five or six coloured yarns in every pile line and lifted whichever one the pattern needed.[@process-loompile-tapestry-brussels] Richard Whytock of Edinburgh printed the whole pattern onto a single pile warp before weaving instead. The Glasgow School of Art Archives say he patented his "Tapestry carpet loom" in 1832; another account dates it to about 1840, and the patent itself has not been found.[@process-loompile-tapestry-brussels]

In Glasgow, James Templeton and William Quiglay went the long way round, patenting an improved chenille method in July 1839. A "weft blanket" is woven in the pattern colours and cut into strips, and each strip is folded into a furry chenille yarn and woven into a backing as the pile. The result is seamless cut pile with not a single knot tied.[@process-loompile-chenille-axminster] In 1876, according to Britannica's student encyclopedia, Halcyon Skinner perfected a power loom for Royal Axminster that made patterned cut-pile carpet cheaper, a claim still to be checked against a second source.[@skinner-axminster-1876]

<Figure id="process-loompile-chenille-axminster" size="margin" caption="Stair-carpet sample by James Templeton of Glasgow, late 19th century, its pile woven in chenille strips." />

However ingenious, each of these looms did what the Pazyryk weaver did: it made the pile and its backing together, in one process.[@process-tufting-overview]

## Pile sewn into a finished cloth

In New England households, meanwhile, rug-makers were starting from the other end, with a cloth already woven. In a yarn-sewn rug, yarn is sewn with a needle in a continuous running stitch through a closely woven cloth, leaving loops on the face that are usually clipped.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs] A hearth rug of 1800–1820 in the Connecticut Historical Society is worked this way in wool on plain-woven linen, with a boy and a girl under a grapevine and roses in the corners.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs] The Metropolitan Museum's bed rugs use the same needle-worked running stitch, "not hooked, as was once assumed", and they are the subject of chapter 2.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs; @met-13-207]

<Figure id="process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs" caption="Yarn-sewn hearth rug, 1800–1820: cut and uncut wool loops on plain-woven linen, with a boy and girl under a grapevine." />

Other makers sewed on cloth instead of yarn. For a chenille-shirred rug, a strip of fabric is stitched lengthwise down the middle and gathered into a caterpillar-like strand, then sewn flat along the pattern; Historic New England's "Apple Tree Farm" rug, from Cogswell's Grant in Essex, Massachusetts, is dated 1820–1830.[@process-sewn-shirred-hne]

Hooking needed a different tool and a different cloth. The maker pulls strips of fabric or yarn up through a woven foundation with a hook, leaving loops on the surface, and the usual foundation is burlap. The Canadian Museum of History says burlap was not sold commercially in North America until the 1850s, and Colonial Williamsburg says the craft was held back until it arrived.[@process-hooking-origins] Where hooking began is still argued. W. W. Kent's *The Hooked Rug* (1930) traced it to Yorkshire, as Wikipedia reports, while a Colonial Williamsburg label places its start in New England and south-east Canada in the 1840s.[@process-hooking-origins; @process-hooking-cw-1983-611-1]

The oldest known Canadian hooked rug is the work of two sisters. Susanna Smith, aged sixteen, designed it, and Abigail, aged thirteen, hooked it in 1860 at New Maryland, near Fredericton, New Brunswick.[@process-hooking-origins] By the early 20th century hooking could also be organised as outwork. The Grenfell Mission in northern Newfoundland and Labrador gave women kits of pre-dyed material and burlap stencilled with designs, then sold the mats to fund its medical work; sources date the start to 1906, 1909 or 1913.[@process-hooking-grenfell] For material, a 1928 appeal asked supporters for their worn stockings,[@process-hooking-grenfell] which the Baltimore Museum of Art says were donated by women in North American and British cities.[@process-hooking-grenfell-bma]

<PullQuote from="process-hooking-grenfell-bma" who="Grenfell Mission slogan, quoted by the Baltimore Museum of Art">When your stockings run, let them run to Labrador!</PullQuote>

Braided and crocheted rugs did without a foundation cloth altogether, coiling a braid of cloth strips or working strips into chained loops with a hook.[@process-braided-hne; @process-crochet-knit-afam] But the yarn-sewn and hooked rugs had already set out the principle that matters for this book: the cloth comes first, and the pile is put into it.

## Tufting

Tufting is that principle at industrial scale. A needle, or a bar of hundreds of needles, pierces a backing that already exists and leaves loops, which can be cut. The tufts are then locked with latex or another adhesive, often with a secondary backing laminated on. The needle does not make the cloth it works in.[@process-tufting-overview]

<Figure id="pat-us2335487a" size="margin" caption="Joe C. Cobble, “Multiple Needle Tufting Machine”, US patent 2,335,487, filed 1941 and granted 1943." />

In 1891 Ebenezer Ross of Toledo, Ohio, patented a two-handled needle-and-gauge tool and called it a "Turfing Implement" (chapter 9).[@pat-us463548a] Fifty years later, in January 1941, Joe C. Cobble and George B. Muse filed for a "Multiple Needle Tufting Machine", granted in November 1943.[@pat-us2335487a] According to the Carpet and Rug Institute, machinery developed for making chenille rugs was then widened to make larger rugs and broadloom carpet, the story of chapters 4 and 5.[@chenille-widened] By one count, still to be traced to its source, 10 per cent of American carpet was tufted in 1950;[@ttw-tufted-share] one textile source now puts tufted products at more than 90 per cent of carpet.[@process-tufting-overview]

Industry has found still other routes since, some with no yarn construction at all. Needle-punching drives barbed needles through a loose web of fibres until they tangle into a felt-like fabric,[@process-industrial-needlepunch] flocked flooring such as Flotex shoots electrically charged nylon fibres end-first into wet adhesive,[@process-industrial-flocked] and fusion bonding sets pile yarn into a layer of adhesive, usually PVC.[@process-industrial-fusion-bonded] Pattern has left the loom too: printing forces dye through stencilled screens, one for each colour, into plain carpet, usually tufted, and later machines inject it from computer-controlled jets.[@process-industrial-printed] Britannica's student encyclopedia still names weaving and tufting as the two main methods of making carpet today.[@process-tufting-overview]

This chapter argues that tufting belongs with the sewn and hooked rugs rather than with the loom. The knotter and the weaver make pile and cloth together; the hearth-rug maker, the hooker and the tufting machine start from a cloth that already exists and put the pile into it.[@process-tufting-overview] That separation of pile from backing is the idea the rest of this book follows.
