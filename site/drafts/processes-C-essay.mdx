In 1891 Ebenezer Ross of Toledo, Ohio, patented a small two-handled tool, a needle with a gauge, and called it a "Turfing Implement".[@pat-us463548a] Fifty years later Joe C. Cobble and George B. Muse filed for a "Multiple Needle Tufting Machine".[@pat-us2335487a] Between the hand tool and the machine lies the subject of this book. Before following it, this chapter places tufting among the other ways of making a rug, because what tufting is becomes clear only beside what it is not.

The clearest way to sort those methods is by where the pile comes from, and rug-makers have given three answers. They have built the pile into the cloth as the cloth was made, by tying knots or by working it on a loom. They have done without pile, in felt and in flat weaves. Or they have taken a cloth that was already finished and put the pile into it. Britannica's student encyclopedia draws the line between the first and the last: in weaving, pile and backing are made together, while tufting adds pile to a backing that already exists.[@process-tufting-overview]

## Pile made with the cloth

The oldest known knotted-pile carpet shows the first answer. Found frozen in Barrow 5 at Pazyryk, in the Altai Mountains, by Sergei Rudenko's excavation in 1949, it is dated by radiocarbon to the 5th to 4th century BCE, though the Hermitage listing dates the barrow to 252–238 BCE.[@process-knotted-pazyryk] Its weaver tied short lengths of wool round pairs of warps, one row at a time, cut them to height and locked each row with wefts.[@process-knotted-pazyryk] The knot varies from tradition to tradition, round two warps, round one and part of the next, or round a single warp,[@process-knotted-knot-types; @process-knotted-spanish-knot] but the method does not: the pile is tied in as the cloth grows. A carpet attributed to Thomas Whitty of the Axminster manufactory in Devon, from about 1765, was still knotted by hand in this way.[@met-928622]

<Figure id="met-928622" size="wide" caption="Carpet attributed to Thomas Whitty, Axminster carpet manufactory, Devon, about 1765: wool, knotted pile." />

On the loom, pile was raised rather than tied, but the principle stayed the same. Brussels carpet raises its worsted pile in loops over steel wires laid between the warps, and Wilton cuts those loops.[@woven-brussels-wire-loop; @wilton-1741] Richard Whytock of Edinburgh printed the pattern onto a single pile warp instead of running five or six colours in every line; the Glasgow School of Art Archives date his patent to 1832, another account to about 1840, and the patent has not been found.[@process-loompile-tapestry-brussels] James Templeton and William Quiglay, in Glasgow, patented an improved chenille method in July 1839, weaving a patterned "weft blanket", cutting it into furry strips and weaving those into a backing as the pile.[@process-loompile-chenille-axminster] Each of these inventions changed how the loom handled pile and colour, and each still made the pile and its backing in one process.[@process-tufting-overview]

## Rugs with no pile

The second answer is older still, if Britannica is right that non-woven felts are considered the first textile goods produced. Felt is fibre matted together by heat, moisture and friction, with no yarn and no adhesive.[@process-felt-definition] The same Pazyryk burials held felt hangings, saddlecloths and cushions, cut and sewn onto felt grounds,[@process-felt-pazyryk-hangings] and Kyrgyz shyrdak makers still cut and sew felt rugs in a craft that UNESCO placed on its Urgent Safeguarding List in 2012.[@process-felt-shyrdak]

Flatweaves are the woven version of the same choice. Kilims, Navajo blankets, dhurries and Swedish rölakan are tapestry-woven, each coloured weft running only where its colour is needed and packed down to hide the warp; Britannica dates the earliest known tapestry to Egyptian linen of 1483–1411 BCE.[@process-flatweave-earliest-tapestry-egypt] A flat weave can be fine, and the Penn Museum's late 19th-century dhurrie from North India carries elephants and horses across its field,[@process-flatweave-dhurrie-penn] but it has no pile to give.

## Pile put into a finished cloth

The third answer is the one this book follows. A yarn-sewn rug is made with a needle, in a continuous running stitch through a closely woven cloth, leaving loops on the face that are usually clipped.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs] A hearth rug of 1800–1820 in the Connecticut Historical Society is worked this way, and the Metropolitan Museum's bed rugs, the subject of chapter 2, use the same stitch, "not hooked, as was once assumed".[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs; @met-13-207]

<Figure id="process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs" caption="Yarn-sewn hearth rug, 1800–1820: cut and uncut wool loops on plain-woven linen, with a boy and girl under a grapevine." />

Hooking works from the same starting point with a different tool, pulling strips of fabric or yarn up through a woven foundation, usually burlap.[@process-hooking-origins] Its spread followed the cloth.[@process-hooking-cw-1983-611-1] The Canadian Museum of History says burlap was not sold commercially in North America until the 1850s, and its oldest known Canadian hooked rug was hooked in 1860 by Abigail Smith, aged thirteen, at New Maryland, New Brunswick.[@process-hooking-origins] By the early 20th century the Grenfell Mission in Newfoundland and Labrador was supplying women with stencilled burlap and pre-dyed material and selling their mats to fund its medical work.[@process-hooking-grenfell]

<PullQuote from="process-hooking-grenfell-bma" who="Grenfell Mission slogan, quoted by the Baltimore Museum of Art">When your stockings run, let them run to Labrador!</PullQuote>

What the sewn and hooked rugs share is an order of work: the cloth was woven first, and the pile was added to it afterwards with a needle or a hook.[@process-sewn-yarn-sewn-chs; @process-hooking-origins]

## Tufting

Tufting is that order of work carried into industry. A needle, or a bar of hundreds of needles, pierces a primary backing and leaves loops, which can be cut; the tufts are then locked with latex or another adhesive, often with a secondary backing laminated on. The tufting needle does not make the cloth it works in.[@process-tufting-overview] Ross's hand tool of 1891 and Cobble and Muse's machine of 1941 both belong to that family,[@pat-us463548a; @pat-us2335487a] and so do the chenille bedspread machines that, according to the Carpet and Rug Institute, were widened to make larger rugs and broadloom carpet (chapters 4 and 5).[@chenille-widened]

<Figure id="pat-us2335487a" size="margin" caption="Joe C. Cobble, “Multiple Needle Tufting Machine”, US patent 2,335,487, filed 1941 and granted 1943." />

The consequences were large. By one count, still to be traced to its source, 10 per cent of American carpet was tufted in 1950;[@ttw-tufted-share] one textile source now puts tufted products at more than 90 per cent of carpet, and Britannica's student encyclopedia names weaving and tufting as the two main methods of making carpet today.[@process-tufting-overview] Industry has also found other routes, matting fibres with barbed needles, shooting nylon fibres into adhesive, or setting yarn into PVC.[@process-industrial-needlepunch; @process-industrial-flocked; @process-industrial-fusion-bonded]

This chapter argues that tufting belongs with the sewn and hooked rugs rather than with the loom. Like them, it puts pile into a cloth that already exists, and that separation of pile from backing is what sets it apart from knotting and weaving.
